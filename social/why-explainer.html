103 people died.
Why won’t they just tell us?

Everyone is asking the same thing. Show us the boat’s papers. Raise the wreck. Say how many died. None of that has happened. Here are six possible reasons why — in the simplest words we can find. Some are innocent. Some are not. The record cannot yet tell them apart, and this page says so honestly.

The government has never said how many people died. It only says how many bodies were found. The rest — about thirty people — appear in no government paper at all.

Six possible reasons

Read them all before deciding. The last one is probably closest to the truth.

REASON 1

There may be no papers to show

A retired ship pilot read Guyana’s sea laws and wrote 253 pages about them. He found that a boat the size of the Barima may fall through a hole in the law.

No rule saying how many life jackets. No rule saying anyone must count the passengers. Maybe no rule requiring the certificate everyone is asking for.

If true: nobody is hiding the paper. The paper was never made.
REASON 2

They picked a story on day one and got stuck with it

One day after the boat sank — before anyone had investigated anything — the minister said the cause in one word: “mischief.” He said the problem was at the wharf, with the workers.

Nine days later, three workers were charged with murder. Nobody above them was charged with anything.

If true: they are protecting a story, not a secret. Changing it now means admitting it goes higher.
REASON 3

Money. The state has to pay, and it never said how much

The boat had no insurance. The government admits it carries the cost itself. About 103 people died.

Families got $350,000 for funerals — and the government said clearly that this is not compensation. Real compensation waits for an inquiry with no finish date.

One expert reads the law as giving families only until about 18 January 2027 to make a claim. Nobody has told them that.

If true: every month of delay costs the state less, and the families more.
REASON 4

A $1.4 billion contract nobody wants examined

In 2022 the government paid out G$1.4 billion to build a new wharf at Port Kaituma. Almost four years later it is still not finished.

Because it is not finished, the newer ferry could not go there. So an 87-year-old boat kept doing the run. The Prime Minister has admitted this himself.

The inquiry’s announced list of things to look at does not name that contract.

If true: the thing that put an old boat on that route is the one thing not being looked at.
REASON 5

They may simply not know how

Guyana has never properly investigated a sea disaster. No trained team. No law setting out how it is done. No system for it at all.

So the government did the only things it knew how to do: send police, charge people, and call an inquiry. It looks like hiding. It may just be emptiness.

If true: not wicked. Just a country with nothing built to handle this.
REASON 6

Most likely: all of it at once

There is probably no single secret buried somewhere.

There is a country whose laws asked for almost nothing. A government that chose a story too quickly. And three expensive reasons never to change that story: ministers’ jobs, a $1.4 billion contract, and a bill for a hundred lives.

You don’t need a conspiracy to get this. You need people who guessed on day one and found it cost more to admit it every day after.

Four papers would end the argument tomorrow

Not opinions. Not theories. Four documents. Each one would settle this in a different direction.

1

The boat’s safety certificate

Does it even exist? How many people was she really allowed to carry? Was 250 life jackets enough?

2

The wharf video

Police took it on day two. It told us how many people boarded. What does it show about the cargo?

3

A real payment plan

With a real amount, real rules, and real money set aside — before the claim deadline passes.

4

The inquiry’s official rules

Not yet published anywhere. Do they include the $1.4bn wharf contract, or not?

Three men have been charged. They have NOT had a trial.

Captain Kevin Price, Chief Mate Rondell Roberts and Goods Superintendent Delon Granderson are accused people. Nothing on this page says they are guilty of anything, and nothing here should be read that way. Notice also that on Reason 5, they may be as much a product of Guyana’s broken sea laws as anyone else.
