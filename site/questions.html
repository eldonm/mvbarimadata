Questions of the record

Analysis by this archive, reasoning over all the documents it holds: what the record establishes, what it cannot, and where accounts cannot both be true.

This page is analysis, not record

Everywhere else this archive reports only what sources say, attributed. Here it reasons and answers directly. Evidence is named and linked; where the record cannot settle a question that is stated. If this page and the record pages ever disagree, the record pages are authoritative. It does not speculate about the intentions of the three men charged on 28 July, who have not been tried.

Everywhere else on this site the archive reports only what published sources say, attributed. Here it reasons over the 209 documents and answers directly. Evidence is named and linked; where the record cannot settle a question, that is stated rather than filled in. The chronology, figures and positions pages hold the record itself, and if they and this page ever disagree, they are right and this page is wrong.

One limit. Three men were charged with murder on 28 July and have not been tried. This page does not speculate about their guilt — nor suggest they are being blamed for others' decisions. Institutional conduct is analysed throughout; individual culpability is left to the court and the inquiry.

1Four capacity figures exist and no certificate. What follows from that?

Four capacity figures are on the record and they do not agree — three of them stated by officials since the sinking, the fourth the vessel's original rating as officials describe it.

Stated by When Cargo Passengers
Minister Edghill 19 July 284 tonnes 397
MARAD Director-General Thomas 21–22 July 126 tonnes 394
MARAD Director-General Thomas 24–25 July 120 tonnes 394
1938 rating, as described by Thomas — 120 tonnes 150

The vessel was manifested at 268 tonnes. An early line-by-line figure from Minister Edghill's own audit was 260 tonnes.

The spread is not a rounding difference. The highest official figure is 2.4 times the lowest. The direction is what matters: if 284 is correct, the manifested load was within limits. If 120 or 126 is correct, the vessel was manifested at more than twice its permitted cargo. The lower figures come from MARAD and the higher from the Ministry; the corpus does not show how either was derived.

Thomas's finding on 22 July that the vessel was "not overloaded" cites the load line rather than the tonnage certificate, and on that basis it is consistent with the tonnage limit he gave. The analytical point is not that he contradicted himself. It is that the overloading question was answered by reference to a waterline observation rather than to a document — and that the tonnage limit he gave is one the manifested load exceeded by roughly a factor of two.

Asked when and on what basis the 1938 rating of 150 passengers became 394, Thomas said: "I don't have that information."

What the record cannot establish: which figure is correct. No survey, load line or passenger capacity certificate appears in any of the 209 documents.

What would settle it: the certificate, and the instrument that authorised the increase from 150 to 394 passengers. No such instrument appears in the corpus, and the regulator's Director-General did not give its basis when asked.

2What does the Mayday contradiction actually turn on?

Five outlets place a distress call at or about 23:00 on 18 July. BBC News gives 23:01 local. Deutsche Welle, CBC News, Ignite News and Kaieteur News each state that air traffic control received it, Kaieteur locating it at the Timehri tower and giving 23:01. That timing is the spine of every published chronology, including this archive's.

These are five published reports, not five independent confirmations. As the method page records, CBC's report is Thomson Reuters copy and CBC has credited Ignite News as the origin of other material in this corpus. Syndication means the underlying number of first-hand accounts is smaller than the number of outlets.

On 25 July the Prime Minister said of a Mayday from the captain: "We have no such record."

The contradiction is narrower than it first appears, and the wording is where it turns. The Prime Minister's statement is about a Mayday from the captain, and about the state's record of one. Three readings fit both sets of facts:

  1. The call's source is not established by the reports. They say a distress call was received; the Prime Minister's statement addresses a call from the captain specifically. Who originated it is not settled by any document in the corpus, and this archive draws no inference from that gap — the conduct of the crew that night is a matter before a court.
  2. It was received by aviation and never entered the maritime record. Four outlets say air traffic control took it. If the Maritime Rescue Coordination Centre logged nothing, a maritime official could truthfully say the state has no such record while a call had in fact been received.
  3. The record exists and was not retrieved before the statement was made.

These have very different implications. The second would mean the country's maritime emergency system did not capture its own worst maritime emergency — a more serious finding than a disputed timestamp, and one that bears directly on the hours that elapsed before any aircraft was over the site.

What would settle it: the MRCC log and the air traffic control recordings for 18–19 July. Janette Bulkan has publicly asked the MSRC to publish the rescue timeline, including the times at which any Mayday was relayed to Exxon offshore vessels, the Omni Helicopters base at Ogle, the police boat near the Pomeroon mouth, the Coast Guard and MARAD's Ocean Pilot boats, and has asked the centre and Timehri radio whether a Pan Pan was issued. She does not ask for the air traffic recordings; this archive does. Neither has been published.

3All three charged are employees of the operator. What does the record show about the regulator and the ministry?

On 28 July the captain, chief mate and goods superintendent were charged with murder. All three are employees of the Transport & Harbours Department. They are accused persons who have not been tried, and nothing here should be read as a view on their guilt or their innocence.

The charging decisions were made on the advice of the Director of Public Prosecutions, an office independent of the ministry, and the DPP's reasoning is not public. This archive alleges no interference and has no basis to.

What can be observed is the boundary of the record as it stands. No charge appears in the corpus against any official of MARAD, the regulator; the basis on which the passenger rating was raised from 150 to 394 has not been published. None appears against any member of the T&HD board, the body the Kaieteur News "Peeping Tom" column identifies as holding operational oversight. None appears against any official in the ministry that owns the fleet.

Two published positions bear on where responsibility above the operator would sit, and they point in opposite directions. Opposition figures have raised the question of loading authority: MP Terrence Campbell has warned that the investigation must not end with lower-level workers being blamed while decisions by senior officials and regulatory agencies escape examination — in his own words, "Accountability must go from top to bottom" — and Opposition Leader Azruddin Mohamed states that loading orders come from senior officials rather than from the staff carrying them out. Both are allegations, attributed, uncorroborated in the corpus, and both bear on matters now before a court; this archive does not evaluate them. Against that, the "Peeping Tom" column argues that the T&HD's own board is what insulates the Minister from operational responsibility, and that a minister's constitutional duty is to set policy direction and secure parliamentary funding, "not to supervise the loading of cargo or verify passenger manifests on a daily basis" — an argument that, if accepted, locates responsibility at the board and the department rather than above them. Both cases are on the positions page at length.

What the record cannot establish: whether charges further up the chain were considered and declined, or are still under consideration. The DPP's reasoning is not public, and the Commission of Inquiry has not sat.

4Was the vessel's deterioration documented before 18 July, and by whom?

Yes. The clearest items are government documents — with one important qualification about how the most-cited of them has been read.

The March 2026 tender. DPI announced on 9 March 2026 that over G$440m would go to rehabilitating three vessels, the Barima's share estimated at G$124.5m. APNU MP Saiku Andrews states that the tender's bill of quantities required examination of approximately 10,000 square metres of hull plating and frames, "with deteriorated sections to be renewed where necessary." The tender file itself is not in the corpus, so its contents rest on his account of it, carried by unbylined CMC copy. And the conditional matters: a bill of quantities specifying that deteriorated sections be renewed where necessary is a specification anticipating deterioration, not a survey establishing it. It has been reported as though it were a finding. It is not one. What it does establish is that four months before the sinking the state was procuring hull work at that scale.

The department's own record. Five DPI releases in the six years before the sinking touch the vessel or its operator, including the March 2026 tender announcement above. Of the rest: June 2021, the Barima sailing again after engine repairs; September 2024, repairs "to be completed soon" on a vessel with "more than 80 years of service"; and — the strongest — March 2025, a DPI release stating that the T&HD is undergoing extensive changes to address longstanding issues of negligence, mismanagement and accountability, and quoting Minister Edghill that destruction of government property through negligence "would no longer be tolerated." The characterisation is DPI's own prose, not Edghill's quoted words, and it is the state describing its own operator. A September 2020 DPI release recording a history of mechanical failures also exists, but this archive holds only a tool-condensed capture of it and does not quote from it.

Outside the state. Eleven years earlier, master mariner Capt. R. E. W. Adams named this vessel directly in a Stabroek News letter of 2 October 2015, citing a Kaieteur News inspection that described its deck, winch and generator area as "a mass of junk and rust," and arguing such a vessel would be barred from sea or scrapped elsewhere in the Caribbean. His attribution matters: he blamed missed dry-docking, not age — the same distinction MARAD later drew, applied to the opposite conclusion.

Against this: Minister Edghill stated there was "no report of any kind that suggested the vessel was unseaworthy," and DPI headlined its release "MV Barima was seaworthy." Those statements and the tender are not necessarily inconsistent — a hull can require scheduled work without being formally reported unseaworthy. But the absence of any survey in the corpus means the assertion of seaworthiness rests on a docking chronology rather than on a certificate.

Still missing: the 19 September 2015 Kaieteur inspection report Adams cites, the March 2026 tender file itself, and the dry-docking history.

5The replacement existed and did not sail. What does the record establish about why?

This is the most completely documented chain in the corpus, and it runs 66 months.

  • January 2021 — the T&HD contracts India's Garden Reach Shipbuilders for the MV Ma Lisha, US$12.7m, on an Indian line of credit. It is larger than the Kimbia and the Barima, so Port Kaituma and Mabaruma must be upgraded to take it.
  • September 2022 — Edghill announces a new Port Kaituma stelling. CEMCO is contracted for design and supervision.
  • 30 December 2022 — construction awarded by public tender to International Import and Supplies, G$1.4bn.
  • August 2023 — the Ma Lisha makes its maiden Region One voyage, to Mabaruma. Not Port Kaituma.
  • January 2024 — Edghill states the stelling is 77% complete, finishing in the third quarter.
  • February 2024 — at about 90%, fissures appear during final casting of pile caps. Work stops. An initial investigation indicates the tie rod strength specified in the design was inadequate; a separate independent third-party review then finds further design deficiencies.
  • July 2024 — the contractor states publicly that the cause was design, not construction.
  • August 2024 — Edghill confirms CEMCO acknowledged design flaws.
  • 27 July 2026 — still unfinished.

What the sequence establishes is narrower than a cause. The Port Kaituma stelling was never completed; a design fault stopped work in February 2024 with the project at about 90 per cent; and across the whole period from the Ma Lisha's commissioning to the sinking, the 87-year-old Barima remained the vessel on the Port Kaituma run. The corpus does not settle why the Ma Lisha never took that route. The Prime Minister attributes its absence to incomplete wharf works; residents attribute it to insufficient cargo capacity. Neither account is tested against the other, and the Ma Lisha had not served Port Kaituma at any point before February 2024 either, so the design fault prolonged an existing incompleteness rather than creating it.

A second thread is contested on the record and should not be stated flat: the Lady Northcote is reported withdrawn in 2019, leaving the Barima alone on the run, but Kaieteur News stated on 24 July that the sister ship "continues operating." The archive flags this as unresolved.

On institutional conduct, two things in the sequence are worth naming without inferring intent. No public acknowledgment of the structural problem appears in the corpus before the contractor's July 2024 statement; the Ministry's confirmation followed in August 2024. The corpus does not show what prompted either statement. And in August 2024 Edghill said of additional costs: "I have no bill." In the twenty-three months since, the corpus contains no stated completion date, no final cost, and no public accounting for the overrun. A project can be delayed by a genuine engineering fault — that is what the evidence here shows — without the same fault explaining the absence of any public accounting afterwards.

6What actually changed in the safety procedures announced in late July?

DPI announced on 24 July that the government had begun tightening ferry safety procedures, and the Prime Minister set out further measures the same morning in a Jump Start radio interview, reported by DPI that day and by the Guyana Chronicle the next. Reading them closely, most of what was announced is enforcement of rules that already existed:

  • strengthened compliance with existing standard operating procedures;
  • stricter adherence to existing ticketing and passenger-management rules;
  • triggered by confirmation that passengers had boarded unticketed.

One announced measure is not enforcement of an existing rule. The Prime Minister said that expanded CCTV coverage and improved monitoring of wharves and loading areas would form part of the reforms being advanced, alongside a commitment to strengthen mandatory testing, port-clearance procedures, supervision and monitoring. That is new capability, and this archive earlier described the package as containing nothing new. That description was wrong and has been corrected here and on the revisions page.

What remains absent from the announcements is any rule change at the point of the failure the state itself identified: no manifest-verification requirement, no headcount protocol, no life-jacket mandate, no hard capacity cap, no night-sailing restriction, no new inspection regime.

The analysis is narrow. A measure directed at compliance addresses execution rather than rulemaking; a measure adding cameras adds observation of execution. Neither changes what a vessel is permitted to carry or what must be checked before it sails. Whether it should have is a question for the Commission, whose announced terms of reference reach both. This archive draws no connection between the content of these measures and any charging decision.

What this archive cannot say: whether new rules were needed. What it can say is which were announced and which were not.

7Which figures moved, when, and on whose authority?
Complement Recovered Rescued Missing
Manifest, 18 Jul 133 — — —
20 Jul 179 27 69 83
24 Jul 179 73 76 30

The 179 first appears in reporting of 20 July, attributed to the government generally and, in CMC copy, to the Prime Minister; the government's National Communications Network is the named source for the 69 rescued and 83 missing. The Prime Minister restated the 179 on 21 July, and it was tied publicly to the boarding-area CCTV review by 24–25 July.

Two findings, one reassuring and one not.

The state's arithmetic is internally consistent. On 20 July, 27 + 69 + 83 = 179. On 24 July, 73 + 76 + 30 = 179. The fall in "missing" from 83 to 30 is fully accounted for by the rise in recoveries and the revision of the survivor count. A drop of 53 in four days can look like a figure being revised away; here the figures reconcile, and this archive says so plainly rather than leaving the impression uncorrected.

The state has never published a death toll. Government releases report bodies recovered. Against a complement of 179 and 76 survivors, subtraction leaves approximately 103 people. Seventy-three bodies have been recovered. The residual of roughly thirty appears in no government document as a figure of the dead — the Prime Minister's phrasing is "unaccounted for."

That distinction was defensible while recovery was ongoing. It is harder to sustain now. On 28 July the Prime Minister stated that "it's obvious that bodies are trapped in the wreck" and that the vessel is "likely to remain there as is," while the President is reported as assuring families that the decision on the wreck "would solely depend on the wishes of the families." In those circumstances the gap between "unaccounted for" and an official death toll is a question the state has not yet been asked to answer.

The complement itself moved from 133 to 179 — a difference of 46 between the paper manifest and the CCTV-derived count. How that gap arose is a matter for the Commission and, in part, for the court; this archive does not characterise it.

8What does the first-response record show about capability, as distinct from effort?

The effort of the responders themselves is not in dispute anywhere in this corpus — though the adequacy of the state's response as a response certainly is, and those criticisms are on the positions page. What follows is about capability, and the clearest evidence for the gap comes from the government itself.

Minister Edghill stated that the early response was hampered by darkness and by a lack of vessels with scanners, leaving rescuers reliant on eyesight and sound. Scanner-equipped vessels were subsequently supplied by oil and gas sector companies. How the wreck was located is contested in the corpus — a fisherman, Roraima Airways reporting coordinates, ExxonMobil seabed mapping and sonar, and the private firm VEHSI are each credited by different outlets, possibly describing sequential stages of the same effort. What is not contested is that the assets that found it were private.

The timeline, with one caveat: reports place a distress call at about 23:00, which is the timing Question 2 examines and which the Prime Minister's office disputes. The first air asset in the record is Capt. Gerry Gouveia's private fixed-wing aircraft, airborne from Ogle at 01:36 — an aircraft that can sight but cannot recover. Survivor Wayne Kitson states he was in the water from about 22:00 until rescued around 04:00. Small private boats pulled at least 17 people from the sea. The 15-member French military team arrived on Monday 20 July, two days after the sinking.

Janette Bulkan has asked publicly why the rescue helicopters and trained rescue swimmers at the Omni Helicopters base at Ogle were never launched, and why the police boat near the Pomeroon mouth and MARAD's two Ocean Pilot boats were not tasked sooner. Two GDF helicopters were reported unserviceable.

The distinction the record forces is between capability and deployment, and it does not resolve it. Assets existed: the Ocean Pilot boats, the police boat, the Ogle base. What the record shows deployed in the first critical hours is civilian boats, one private fixed-wing aircraft, and sight and sound. Whether the state assets were tasked, and when, is precisely the open question — and it is the question the MRCC log would answer.

What would settle the timeline: the MRCC log, requested publicly and not published.

9What has the state published about this disaster, and what has it not?

This is the clearest pattern in the corpus.

Published, prolifically. Seventeen Department of Public Information releases dated 19–25 July, out of twenty-two DPI items in the corpus overall. Forty articles in the state-owned Guyana Chronicle. Ministerial Facebook posts, presidential video statements, televised briefings. Sixty-two of the 209 documents here — about three in ten — are the state's own voice.

Not published. Any certificate of survey, load line or passenger capacity. Any official death toll. Any gazetted instrument establishing the Commission of Inquiry. Any MARAD advisory or notice to mariners. Any Auditor General finding specific to the T&HD. The MRCC timeline. The March 2026 tender file. Any police or government statement on the three women detained at the national prayer service. Hansard for the July sitting.

The suspension of the loading and dispatch management team was announced by Facebook post, and described as protecting the integrity of the investigation rather than as a finding against anyone. The declaration of national mourning was communicated primarily through social media, though a formal DPI release for it does exist.

The asymmetry is the finding: abundant official communication, and no primary document released about the vessel or the response. The one primary instrument published is the declaration of national mourning. Ten days of continuous official output, from 19 to 28 July, has produced no single primary document about the vessel's certification, its capacity basis, or the emergency response timeline.

Two limits on that finding, both real. The corpus can show what was published; it cannot show what exists in a file. And non-publication has explanations that are neither concealment nor incapacity: documents may sit with the Director of Public Prosecutions or await the Commission, and material touching charges now before a court may be withheld for that reason. What remains, after those allowances, is that a state able to publish seventeen releases in seven days has published no certificate — and whether that is because the certificate is being withheld, because it cannot be found, or because it does not exist, the corpus cannot determine. The three call for very different remedies.

10What does the Commission need that is not currently public?

Drawn from the gaps identified across this archive:

  1. The survey, load line and passenger capacity certificates — and the instrument that raised the rating from 150 passengers to 394.
  2. The MRCC log and air traffic control recordings for 18–19 July.
  3. The March 2026 tender file and bill of quantities.
  4. T&HD board minutes, and the documented chain of authority for loading decisions.
  5. The passenger manifest, and the Online Booking System and Mobile Money records behind it.
  6. The dry-docking history, particularly the 2017 refit and the 2024 docking.
  7. The CEMCO design, the independent review that found it deficient, and the revised design.
  8. Auditor General reports touching the T&HD.
  9. The toxicology results and their chain of custody.
  10. The vessel itself.

The last item is the one with a clock on it. On 28 July the Prime Minister stated that no one has been contacted to raise the vessel and that it is "likely to remain there as is." A draft Declaration of Agreement circulated to families three days earlier asks them to accept the wreck as an undisturbed memorial. Relatives have said publicly that they fear this would obscure physical evidence before the Commission can inspect the hull.

The hull is the primary physical evidence of loading, stowage, watertight integrity and structural condition. The commissioners were named on 26 July; the establishing instrument is not gazetted; the commission has not sat. A decision about the wreck may therefore fall to be taken before the body appointed to investigate it is constituted. The government's stated position is that the letters are "just for discussion" and that the decision will follow the wishes of the families. This archive takes no view on what should be decided; it records that the order in which these things happen is itself a question the Commission may wish to ask.

11When the government's account has changed, has it always changed in the same direction?

Not always. Two changes ran against the government's position and the rest did not; the difference is in how each came about.

Nine changes are set out below. They are the clearest in this corpus, and they are not offered as a complete count. Six moved toward a position more favourable to the government, two moved against it, and one is genuinely mixed. The two that moved against it both came in answer to direct questions.

The two that ran against the government. The state's stated cargo limit fell from Minister Edghill's 284 tonnes on 19 July to MARAD Director-General Thomas's 126 tonnes on 21–22 July to 120 tonnes on 24–25 July. Each step made the 268 tonnes on the manifest look worse, and Demerara Waves records that the last revision came only "after he was pressed on whether the passenger capacity was 150." Separately, the Prime Minister conceded on 25 July that "the delay has contributed to us not being able to use the MV Ma Lisha to journey from Georgetown to Port Kaituma" — an admission that a stalled wharf contract kept an 87-year-old vessel on the route. He said it in answer to a question about whether the delay had contributed to the deaths. Nothing in this corpus records him returning to it.

What moved the other way. The explanation of cause moved from a wave and the tide to crew impairment and ground-level breach, and the first version was never withdrawn. "MV Barima was seaworthy" began on 20 July as an absence-of-reports claim in Edghill's own words — "there was no report of any kind that suggested the vessel was unseaworthy" — and by 25 July had hardened in state media into an affirmative determination, the vessel "subsequently deemed seaworthy," on no new document. The salvage position moved from continuing "until every possible avenue has been exhausted" to the vessel being "likely to remain there as is." Suspensions announced on 20 July became "administrative leave" and not dismissal by 25 July. The deployment count went from 22 assets on 23 July to 17 in a DPI release of 24 July headlined "Search and recovery efforts intensify as 73 bodies recovered" — though the Guyana Chronicle was still carrying 22 that same day, so the two figures ran side by side rather than one simply replacing the other. And on 26 July, six days after the state's own revision to 179, the state-owned Guyana Chronicle twice described the vessel as having sailed with "116 passengers, 17 crew members and cargo."

One is genuinely mixed. The revision of the complement from 133 to 179 was unfavourable — it conceded the state did not know who was aboard. It was also arithmetically unavoidable once thirty-two rescued people turned out not to be on the manifest. Its effect was to place the discrepancy at the point of ticketing and loading.

What never moved at all. Three claims held while everything around them shifted: that the vessel was not overloaded; that the Transport & Harbours Department is the operator and MARAD the regulator, so the failure was operational rather than regulatory; and that the government has been transparent. Each is a claim on which the operational-rather-than-regulatory account depends.

The reading that runs the other way. Figures in a mass-casualty event are preliminary by nature, and revision is what responsible authorities do. Search assets fall as recovery scales down; that is not concealment. The 22 and the 17 were published on the same day by two different state outlets, which is at least as consistent with poor coordination between them as with anything designed. And the Guyana Chronicle reverting to the manifest figure on 26 July is exactly what a newsroom working from an old cutting would produce. None of these requires a decision by anyone.

In summary. The record does not support a claim that the government has revised only in self-serving directions — it has twice conceded something damaging. What it supports is narrower and is a statement about effect, not about intention: the changes the state made unprompted are ones whose effect places responsibility at operator level, and the two that ran against its own position came in answer to direct questions and have not been returned to.

12The cause was named in public within twenty-four hours. Who named it, and what has been named since?

The vessel sank late on Saturday 18 July. By Sunday 19 July, before any investigation had reported, a cause had been named in public and three others had been ruled out. Both came from the same minister.

What was ruled out on 19 July, by Minister Edghill. Mechanical failure: "there was no engine problem or any mechanical problem." Water ingress: "it had nothing to do with… taking in water." And overloading, which he described as "misinformation and something to create panic and public mischief," citing a licensed capacity of 284 tonnes against 268 manifested. The following day DPI published "MV Barima was seaworthy – Min Edghill," which is broader than the sentence it rests on.

What was named. Speaking to the children of a missing crew member, he said: "It's a big wave. That's all we hear." And: "It had to do with the tide." Each is double-attested — the wave line by CMC and Caribbean National Weekly, which gives it in the slightly fuller form "From what we hear, it's a big wave"; the tide line by CMC and the Associated Press, which records that the basis was statements from rescued passengers. He added that a full investigation would determine the cause.

By that evening the explanation had changed. At a press conference he disclosed that crew members had tested positive for cannabis, and — asked what sort of operation could have produced the manifest discrepancy — answered: "One word… we can't excuse it: mischief. Mischief. And that's why I'm outraged." Two honest qualifications belong here. That sentence appears in one document in this corpus, a Kaieteur News report of the briefing; it is not corroborated elsewhere. And the question he was answering was about the falsified manifest, not about what capsized the ship. The generalisation of "mischief" from a ticketing fraud to the cause of the sinking happened first in the reporting and then in the political argument. It should not be laid at his door without that qualification.

What he did say about the structure, as Kaieteur reported his explanation rather than quoted it, was that the Transport & Harbours Department is the service provider and MARAD the regulator, and that the disaster stemmed from operational breaches at ground level rather than from regulatory failure. The Guardian carries a genuinely verbatim fragment from the same event: "We are talking about operational breaches; people will be dealt with." At the same briefing MARAD's Director-General stated that a vessel with positive stability rights itself even at twenty degrees of heel, and made a further remark about a captain who "may be compromised by whatever." It is recorded on the positions page; nothing is inferred from it here, and the conduct of the crew that night is a matter before a court.

What has been named since. Very little that is new, and nothing above the wharf. The Prime Minister called the manifest gap "a breakdown in the system." The Vice-President said "there are going to be consequences for people who did this." MARAD's Director-General ruled out overloading and said age was not determinative. On 25 July the Prime Minister restated the operator-and-regulator division. On 28 July the captain, chief mate and goods superintendent were charged with murder at common law on the advice of the Director of Public Prosecutions. No official statement anywhere in this corpus attributes any part of the cause to a decision taken above the crew and the loading staff — with the single partial exception of the Prime Minister's concession about the Port Kaituma wharf, which he framed as a reason the Barima was on the route rather than a reason it sank.

President Ali said on 21 July that now was not the time for speculation or the politicisation of a national tragedy, and the Prime Minister, in a radio interview on 22 July, cautioned against drawing conclusions before investigators finished. Neither had named a cause, and neither is recorded anywhere in this corpus correcting or distancing from the attributions made on 19 July.

The most direct published criticism of this is not from the opposition. The Kaieteur News editorial of 24 July: "before any serious investigation had even begun, Public Works Minister Juan Edghill confidently declared on Sunday that the vessel had not been overloaded… Those assurances were not based on an independent inquiry. They were based on information fed to him by the very system now shown to have failed spectacularly." And: "Instead of acting as the chief investigator, Mr. Edghill became the chief defender of a regulatory framework that has since collapsed under the weight of its own contradictions." The Transparency Institute of Guyana put the structural objection: a minister cannot publicly prejudge the facts an independent investigation exists to determine and then preside over that investigation.

Against that. The corpus holds a sustained argument the other way, and it belongs here. The Kaieteur News "Peeping Tom" column of 27 July holds that the cause is not yet established, that a minister is not culpable for a department's operational faults where that department has its own board, and that the right course is to await the inquiry. Haseef Yusuf, in a letter to Guyana Times, argues that age is not the same as unseaworthiness. And Minister Edghill's own 19 July statements were made alongside the assurance that a full investigation would determine what happened — he did not claim to have concluded one.

The analytical point. The terms of reference announced on 25–26 July cover seaworthiness, loading, possible overcrowding, weather and tides, maintenance history, and crew competence. They cover, in substance, the same questions the minister answered publicly on 19 July. Naming a cause early is not evidence of bad faith; ministers are asked questions and answer them, and this one said in the same breath that an investigation would settle it. What can be said without inferring anything is that the first public account of the cause came from the minister responsible for the fleet, that it excluded regulatory failure, and that an inquiry now has to reach its own view on questions the public already has an answer to.

A great deal of public money was committed to this route. The question is what it delivered.

G$1.4bn (awarded 30 Dec 2022) Port Kaituma stelling construction 77% complete Jan 2024; ~90% Feb 2024, then halted; still unfinished 27 July 2026
G$150.6m (2017) + G$90m (2022) MV Barima hull rehabilitation and emergency repairs Done; vessel returned to service
G$124.5m (engineer's estimate, March 2026) MV Barima docking and overhaul Tendered 9 March 2026. Whether awarded or performed is not established
~US$4m (2026) Kalliopi N, assigned to Region One Arrived May 2026; still uncommissioned when the Barima sank

The savings that were earmarked for this exact wharf. When DPI announced the new ferry in June 2021 it also stated that the government had saved G$588m on the award, and that the saving "will fund new stellings construction at Morawhanna, Kumaka, and Port Kaituma in Region One." Morawhanna and Kumaka were completed — Minister Edghill said so on 22 July 2026. Port Kaituma was not. It is a matter of record that the money was announced for three wharves and that the one serving the route where the vessel sank is the one still unbuilt.

What happened at Port Kaituma. The fullest account in this corpus is a Kiskadee Watch chronology of 27 July, and it is the only document naming the parties; what follows is that chronology's account. On it, the construction contract went by public tender on 30 December 2022 to International Import and Supplies at G$1.4bn, with CEMCO separately contracted for design and supervision. In February 2024, at about ninety per cent complete, fissures appeared during the final casting of pile caps and work stopped. An initial investigation indicated that the tie rod strength specified in the design was inadequate; a separate independent third-party review then found further design deficiencies. In July 2024 the contractor stated publicly that the cause was design rather than construction practice. The next step is independently attributable: in August 2024 the Minister himself confirmed that CEMCO had acknowledged design flaws, and said of the additional costs: "At this particular time, based upon what we are doing, I have no bill, and take my word — at this particular time, I have no bill for additional costs." Approaching four years after the award, the corpus contains no completion date, no final cost and no disciplinary outcome — Kiskadee Watch says as much in terms — and no record of any penalty, termination or re-tender either. That is not an allegation. It is the absence of an accounting.

The March 2026 repairs. DPI announced on 9 March 2026 that over G$440m would go to docking and overhauling three vessels, the Barima's share estimated at G$124.5m, under national competitive bidding. Five companies bid, reportedly between about G$105m and G$124m. Whether the contract was awarded, to whom, and whether any work was done before 18 July is answered nowhere in this corpus. The Opposition Leader alleged on 22 July that "up to today no work was done on the vessel" — a single, uncorroborated opposition claim, recorded as his. Demerara Waves put the question plainly on the same day and it has not been answered: "was that contract ever awarded? Was that work ever done?"

The vessel was uninsured. Minister Edghill confirmed on 22 July that the state carried the financial risk because the ferry was not insured, and that this is long-standing practice for state-owned vessels; the Prime Minister corroborated the policy the same day. Neither stated whether any dedicated fund or self-insurance mechanism exists. The consequence is that replacement, the lost government cargo aboard, and any eventual compensation fall on the treasury as a discretionary decision rather than on an insurer as a claim. No government figure described as compensation appears anywhere in this corpus. The only figures are the opposition's: a demand of no less than G$1m per victim for funeral costs, against a report that some families were offered G$300,000. The Prime Minister's stated position is that payments made so far are "not deemed compensation" but the government's responsibility to assist, and that compensation will be determined after the Commission reports.

What the money bought, stated without inference. A vessel that never served the port. A wharf that stopped at ninety per cent and was never finished. A second vessel that arrived in May and was assigned to this route; no record of its commissioning appears in this corpus before the sinking, and the Minister said on 22 July it would be put into operation "soon". Two rounds of repairs that kept an 87-year-old hull on the route, and a third that was tendered four months before it sank with no published record of whether it happened. And a hull carried at the state's own risk, so that any compensation falls to be decided by government rather than paid by an insurer.

14Will this Commission of Inquiry be able to compel anyone to do anything?

On the evidence of this corpus, that question cannot be answered — and the fact that it cannot is the finding.

Nothing in 209 documents states what powers this Commission holds. Not whether it can summon a witness, take evidence on oath, compel the production of a document, or treat refusal as contempt. One outlet reports that it was appointed under section 2(1) of the Commissions of Inquiry Act; no document quotes the Act, and no instrument has been published. Every reference to summoning witnesses and compelling documents in this corpus attaches to powers people are asking for, not powers anyone reports the Commission as having. The government's own language is consistently discretionary: the President's terms of reference say the Commission is empowered to "determine whether" and to recommend; the Prime Minister says the government will "act upon its recommendations." No government source in this corpus attributes to the Commission a single verb of compulsion.

As of 28 July, the Commission has not been formally constituted. The President announced it by video on 25 July and named five commissioners on 26 July. On the instruments, one report gives his words as: "The necessary legal instruments required to give effect to this decision are currently being formalised. Upon completion, the Commission will commence its work in accordance with the provisions of the law." That quotation appears in a single document; the fuller-fidelity account renders the same point as reported speech. Terms of reference were announced in detail on 25 and 26 July and are set out on the positions page. But nothing has been gazetted, and there is no secretary, no reporting deadline, no budget, no statement on whether hearings will be public, and no commitment that the report will be published. The only procedural statement the government has made is that "the commission will determine its own procedures and method of work" — which places every one of those questions with a body that cannot yet meet.

A route with those powers was proposed. What became of it is not established. On Friday 24 July, APNU's Dr Terrence Campbell wrote to the Speaker seeking an emergency debate under Standing Order 12 on establishing an inquiry by the National Assembly. His proposed terms of reference were reported in specific terms: the commission "would be authorised to summon witnesses, compel the production of documents and examine the conduct of everyone involved in the ferry's maintenance, regulation, loading, operation and final clearance," and would be "empowered to recommend criminal charges, civil proceedings or disciplinary action." He also wanted the Assembly, not the executive alone, to fix its composition, timeframe and budget.

The sequence after that is a matter of dates. The motion went to the Speaker on Friday. The President announced his own commission on Saturday. He named its members on Sunday. Parliament sat on Monday, passed a supplementary budget and several bills, and — on the only report of that sitting in this corpus — the Standing Order 12 motion is not recorded as debated, ruled on, or mentioned. What happened to it is not established here. Whether it was refused, withdrawn or simply never called, this archive cannot say.

Who says the connection is deliberate, and who does not. Campbell predicted it in advance: "the President's move to appoint his own commission is a deliberate ploy to avoid parliamentary debate because I suspect the Speaker would now say that this is not important." A joint statement of five opposition parties noted the announcement came "immediately before the National Assembly is due to debate opposition motions." Kiskadee Watch characterised the appointment as pre-empting the motion. Against that: no government source in this corpus explains why the executive route was chosen, addresses the motion, or answers the objection that it was not consulted. The silence is not evidence of a motive. It is an absence where a rationale would be.

The objection is not only from the opposition. The Guyana Human Rights Association put the structural point: appointment by the authorities with the most to lose from an impartial probe undermines confidence from the outset. Its proposed remedies are worth reading as a diagnosis of exactly this gap — it wants "a positive duty of candour… imposed on public officials and authorities, compelling them to cooperate and testify truthfully, backed by sanctions," state-funded legal representation for bereaved families, and the chair rather than the agencies under investigation to rule on any claim that evidence is shielded. The GHRA is proposing a duty of candour backed by sanctions, which indicates how it reads the present position; it is not evidence of what the instrument contains. The Transparency Institute of Guyana wants the International Maritime Organization to lead instead, "not a domestic Commission of Inquiry under government control," and names the precedent it fears: after the 2023 Mahdia dormitory fire a presidential commission followed, and families were still in the High Court years later. The Opposition Leader has said that reports of previous commissions were not published, and wants this one livestreamed and released in full. He names no specific commission, and neither does anyone else in this corpus, so that charge stands as an assertion rather than a documented pattern.

Against all of this, the government's case for the body it created is on the record. Five commissioners, four of them foreign, described by the President as "carefully selected for their independence, technical expertise, and proven ability", and including a forensic naval architect whose prior casualty work covers the Estonia, the Derbyshire and the Sewol. The Prime Minister's offer on 25 July: "If contacting the International Maritime Organization will help in this inquiry, we will do it." And the Shipping Association of Guyana, the only trade body to take a position in this corpus, welcoming the Commission on 26 July as "an important step toward establishing the facts surrounding this devastating tragedy". None of that speaks to powers of compulsion, which is the question here — but it is the answer the government has given on independence.

One thing the Commission may need to reach is already moving. On 28 July the Prime Minister said the wreck is "likely to remain there as is," while a draft agreement circulated to families three days earlier asks them to accept it as an undisturbed memorial. No document in this corpus states whether the Commission could intervene in a decision about the wreck. By the same date three employees of the operator had been charged on the advice of the Director of Public Prosecutions, an office independent of the ministry, while the body appointed to examine institutional failure had not been constituted. This archive draws no connection between those two facts and takes no view on the charges.

15Which disclosures did the state make within a day, and which has it not made at all?

This question is about disclosure conduct only. Three men were charged with murder on 28 July and have not been tried; nothing here bears on their guilt or innocence, and the toxicology is not treated as evidence of anything.

The speed on one side. The vessel sank late on Saturday 18 July. By the Sunday evening press conference — roughly twenty hours later — the Minister of Public Works had disclosed the results of drug tests carried out on crew members and stated that three ranks were in police custody at Anna Regina. The Prime Minister said the same to reporters that day. Nobody had been charged. The Vice-President later described how the samples were obtained: "when they were seeking medical attention to take some blood from them." On the reporting of that briefing the disclosure was volunteered rather than extracted; the questions from the floor went to the mechanics of the sinking.

By 22 July the loading and dispatch management team had been suspended by Facebook post, and a Transport & Harbours superintendent had been publicly identified by role and circumstance — invited to the Criminal Investigations Department, attending with his lawyer, and detained. HGPTV noted the position on 24 July: "Neither had been charged when their detention was publicly disclosed." On 28 July the charges were announced in a police press release, and the names, ages and home addresses of the three men appeared in the reporting of it.

The speed on the other side. Over the same ten days, the state published no certificate of survey, no load line certificate, no passenger capacity certificate, no maintenance or dry-docking record, no rescue coordination log, no air traffic recording, no tender file, no board minute, and no Auditor General finding. The seaworthiness of the vessel is asserted on a docking chronology rather than a document. Asked when and on what basis the 1938 passenger rating of 150 became 394, MARAD's Director-General said: "I don't have that information." Janette Bulkan asked publicly on 25 July for the rescue timeline and for the times at which any distress call was relayed to named assets. Nothing has been produced, and no refusal has been recorded either. The one primary operational record that was released is the manifest — a document about individuals.

The state has argued that the first fact proves its transparency. This is not an inference. On 22 July the state-owned Guyana Chronicle set out the government's defence: the test "results were immediately made public before the individuals were arrested… Officials stressed that these actions demonstrate the government's commitment to transparency rather than concealment." DPI made the same argument the day before. The Prime Minister's formulation on 21 July was: "I want to reassure you that we have nothing to hide. I believe and I live for transparency."

So the speed of publishing medical results about identifiable employees is offered as the evidence of institutional openness, in the same period in which no institutional record was released at all. That is the asymmetry, and it is the government's own framing that makes it visible.

Two further things the record shows. First, the official accounts of the testing are mutually inconsistent and have never been reconciled. The corpus holds at least four versions of who tested positive — the captain and the first engineer, the captain and the mate (this one in DPI's own release), "two crew members" unspecified, and the captain and two crew members. Detention counts vary between two, three and four. Nobody has corrected any of it.

Second, the fairness caveats are thin but not absent. The Prime Minister did, on 23 July, defend the use of administrative leave as standard practice during an investigation and say it should not be interpreted as a finding of guilt — the one statement of that kind by an official in this corpus. Most of the rest is conditional, that wrongdoing where established will meet the full force of the law, or procedural, that suspension protects the inquiry's integrity. The plainest statement of the presumption comes from outside government, in an anonymous Demerara Waves column of 22 July: "a positive test is not yet a conviction, and I have no interest in condemning men before the law does."

Above the operator, there is nothing to compare it with. Employees of the Transport & Harbours Department have been tested, identified by rank, suspended, detained and charged. Nothing in this corpus records any minister, ministry official, MARAD officer or member of the T&HD board being tested, suspended, placed on leave, detained, or having personal information published in connection with this disaster. Nor has any statement been issued about the three women detained at the government's own national prayer service on 22 July, who say they were arrested for holding signs and later received an apology; Kaieteur News recorded on 24 July that neither government officials nor police had issued any public statement on it, and none has appeared since.

What this does not establish. It does not establish that the documents are being withheld rather than that they cannot be found, and it does not establish any motive. It establishes a pattern in what the state was fast about. Asked to demonstrate transparency, the government pointed to the speed with which it published medical information about identifiable employees — and the records that would answer whether the vessel should have been at sea have not been published at all.

How this page will grow

These fifteen questions were set by the archive. The intention is that future questions come from the public, and are answered here on the same terms: reasoning from the corpus, evidence named, limits stated.

To submit a question, use the contact route on the method and gaps page with "Question" in the subject line. Questions will be published with their answers. Questions that cannot be answered from the record will be published as such, because a question the archive cannot answer is itself a finding about what is missing.
