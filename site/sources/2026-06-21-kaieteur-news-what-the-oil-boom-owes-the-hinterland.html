What the Oil Boom owes the Hinterland

Kaieteur News · Dr. Karen Abrams, MBA, AA

What this source establishes

In a Kaieteur News column published 28 days before the sinking, Dr Karen Abrams argues that oil wealth is concentrating on the coastal strip where 90% of Guyana's population lives, while hinterland communities, home to most Indigenous Guyanese, remain connected only by rivers, rough roads and expensive flights. She cites a joint OECD and Inter-American Development Bank report describing Indigenous Guyanese as the most economically disadvantaged group, warns of rural-to-urban migration altering the social landscape within a generation, and notes that STEMGuyana closed seven hinterland learning pods for lack of funding.

Key claims

  • 90% of Guyana's population lives on the narrow coastal strip where oil benefits first land
  • The interior, home to most Indigenous Guyanese, is connected only by rivers, rough roads and expensive flights
  • Cites a joint OECD / Inter-American Development Bank report on Indigenous Guyanese as the most economically disadvantaged group
  • Warns of rural-to-urban migration altering the social landscape within a generation
  • STEMGuyana had to close seven hinterland learning pods for lack of funding

Quotations

  • “Indigenous Guyanese, roughly ten percent of our population and residing mostly in hinterland areas, remain the most economically disadvantaged group”
  • “Two Guyanas entered this decade. The work of the decade is to leave it as one.”

Prior warnings recorded

  • Dr. Karen Abrams, Kaieteur News, 21 June 2026 — published FOUR WEEKS BEFORE the MV Barima sank. Warned that Guyana's oil wealth is concentrated on the coastal strip where 90% of the population lives, while the interior remains connected only by 'rivers, rough roads, and expensive flights'; that Indigenous Guyanese, about 10% of the population and mostly resident in hinterland areas, 'remain the most economically disadvantaged group' (citing a joint OECD / Inter-American Development Bank report); and that continued neglect risks rural-to-urban migration that could alter the nation's social landscape within a generation. Concludes: 'Two Guyanas entered this decade. The work of the decade is to leave it as one.' This is a hinterland-connectivity warning, not a maritime-safety warning, but it is the closest thing located to a contemporaneous published warning about the structural conditions that produced the disaster.
