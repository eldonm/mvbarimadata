PERSPECTIVE: Built for 1939, sunk in 2026: The MV Barima and the contempt we reserve for our own people

Demerara Waves · Anonymous

Read the original at Demerara Waves ↗

What this source establishes

A Demerara Waves perspective essay assembling the documentary record on the vessel: built 1939 by Ferguson Brothers at Port Glasgow, ordered through the Crown Agents for the Colonies, youngest of three sisters after the Pomeroon (1936) and Lady Northcote (1937), 126 feet with a 29-foot beam at about eight and a half knots. It cites G$150.6 million spent in 2017 and a G$124.5 million engineer's estimate tendered in March 2026 with five bidders, against an G$11.2 billion river-transport budget. It records the Kalliopi N sitting idle awaiting a presidential commissioning, and reports the Coast Guard lacked vessels with proper scanning equipment until oil and gas companies lent theirs.

Figures cited in this source

77 rescued, 41 bodies recovered, 83 still missing (as of 21 July); total rescued/recovered/missing past 170 vs 133 claimed on manifest

Key claims

  • As of Tuesday 21 July 2026 authorities reported 77 rescued, 41 bodies recovered and 83 still missing.
  • The author notes the combined tally of rescued, recovered and missing had already passed 170 souls, outrunning the 133 the manifest claimed were aboard.
  • The MV Barima was launched in 1939, built on the Clyde in Port Glasgow, Scotland by Ferguson Brothers, ordered through the Crown Agents for the Colonies for the Transport and Harbours Department of British Guiana Railways.
  • She was the youngest of three sister ships - the Pomeroon (1936), the Lady Northcote (1937) and the Barima.
  • The vessel was roughly 126 feet long with a beam of 29 feet, cruising at about eight and a half knots.
  • The Lady Northcote was retired in 2019 and the Barima carried the whole route alone for another seven years.
  • The MV Ma Lisha was meant to replace her in 2023 and did not.
  • The Kalliopi N, built to carry 264 passengers, 82 cars and 19 trucks, arrived in Guyana in May and was assigned to the Region One route but sat waiting on a presidential commissioning that never came in time.
  • The Barima was rehabilitated in 2017 at a cost of G$150.6 million.
  • In March 2026 the Government tendered again for her docking and rehabilitation with an engineer's estimate near G$124.5 million, covering hull plating, structural frames, propulsion, steering, cargo hatch and seawater valves; five contractors bid.
  • The author asks whether that contract was ever awarded and whether the work was ever done.
  • Combined spending on the 1939 hull exceeds G$275 million, inside a river-transport budget of G$11.2 billion this year.
  • The author notes drug testing of the captain and first engineer happened after the vessel capsized, and asks where random pre-departure screening was.
  • The Transport and Harbours Department says its digital ferry-pass relies on manual inputs at district checkpoints - a 'vulnerability' it intends to fix by buying scanners.
  • Edghill rejected overloading and mechanical failure as causes, insisting the vessel carried 268 tonnes against a licensed 284 and was licensed for more than 300 passengers.
  • The Coast Guard reached the site around half past two in the morning, hampered by darkness and a shortage of vessels with proper scanning equipment until oil and gas companies lent theirs.

Quotations

  • “Dozens of our people boarded that vessel as ghosts: uncounted, unregistered and, when the water came, unsearched-for.”— Anonymous, columnist
  • “We do not have a shortage of rules in this country. We have a shortage of anyone enforcing them until the funerals begin.”— Anonymous, columnist
  • “They knew the door was open. They waited for tragedy to close it.”— Anonymous, columnist
  • “reactivity is the most expensive way to govern”— Anonymous, columnist
  • “These were Indigenous and hinterland Guyanese”— the citizens we are quickest to celebrate in speeches and slowest to serve in budgets. — Anonymous, columnist
