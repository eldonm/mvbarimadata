What this source establishes

A one-hour OGGN Oil Talk podcast streamed live on 30 July on the Kaieteur Radio Guyana channel, hosted by Janette Bulkan with David Patterson, AFC shadow minister for public works and a former Minister of Public Works. Patterson, having held the ministry, treats annual certification of these vessels as routine — "how can you say you don't have the registration certificate... but you are certifying it every year" — which bears directly on the certificate no one has produced. He states the Transport and Harbours Department has not turned a profit since 2007 or 2009 and has not been officially audited since about 2005-2006. He cites Cap. 49:07 s. 18, under which inspectors of shipping may prevent overloaded vessels sailing and detain unfit ones. He argues the murder charges hollow out the inquiry because the operator's own management will also decline to self-incriminate, and predicts masters may refuse to sail. Bulkan puts the rescue timeline directly: first rescue at 2:27 a.m., more than three hours on. Auto-caption source; 92 per cent of the programme captured.

Key claims

  • PROGRAMME. A one-hour podcast, streamed live on 30 July 2026 on the Kaieteur Radio Guyana channel, 734 views at retrieval. Host Janette Bulkan; guest David Patterson, shadow minister for public works for the Alliance For Change and a former Minister of Public Works. Stated topic: "beyond the MV Barima ferry wreck, suggestions for sea and river transport systems in Guyana." OGGN describes itself as founded in 2017, a registered 501(c)(3) non-profit in New York, and "fiercely independent with no political affiliations."
  • [SINGLE SOURCE — and directly on the certificate this archive has been unable to find] Patterson, a former Minister of Public Works, states that these vessels are certified annually and compares the process to a road-fitness certificate: "how can you say you don't have the registration certificate of a document... but you are certifying it every year." He adds that speed boats and cargo boats renew their licences at MARAD every year, and that a survey may be partial rather than a full check. If correct, an annual certification record should exist for the Barima.
  • [SINGLE SOURCE] Patterson states that the Transport and Harbours Department "has not turned a profit since 2007 or 2009", and that it has not been officially audited by the Auditor General's office since "most likely I think 2005, 2006". This archive has separately recorded that no Auditor General finding specific to the T&HD could be located; this is a former minister asserting that no official audit has been done for roughly two decades. Unverified.
  • STATUTORY POWERS THAT WERE AVAILABLE. Patterson cites the Shipping Casualties Investigation and Prevention Act, Cap. 49:07, and states that under section 18 inspectors of shipping have "sweeping powers": to board vessels, inspect machinery and equipment, examine certificates, "prevent overloaded vessels from sailing", and detain any vessel unfit for a voyage, with power to remove excess cargo or passengers. He is describing powers that existed before 18 July.
  • [SINGLE SOURCE — extends the self-incrimination problem well beyond the three accused] Patterson argues the murder charges will hollow out the Commission of Inquiry, because the operator's management will also decline to answer: the marine superintendent "is not going to incriminate himself", nor the chief engineer responsible for the vessels, "nor would the general manager of transport and harbours". On his account the inquiry is left with "circumstantial" evidence and "he say, she say".
  • [SINGLE SOURCE] Patterson states a consequence for the service itself: "every captain now on any one of these vessels... may refuse to work", because the charges establish that a captain is ultimately responsible "irrespective of whatever orders you have" and can be charged with murder. He predicts push-back from masters. Bulkan puts the same point as a long-term risk to indigenous and business travel on a route with no road alternative.
  • THE RESCUE TIMELINE, PUT DIRECTLY. Bulkan states that on the Prime Minister's own account the first rescue "took place at 27 minutes past 2 on Sunday, that's more than 3 hours later", with the vessel down roughly seven to ten miles from shore, and asks what is made of a gap of "two and a half hours, no rescue". This is the sharpest published statement of the rescue-timeline question in this corpus.
  • [SINGLE SOURCE] Patterson describes the pre-departure procedure he says applied: a travel plan is issued before sailing, and the "lighthouse", which he calls the maritime nerve centre, together with the harbour master, is supposed to check it. He states that on a Mayday the lighthouse holds a shipping chart showing vessels off the coast and would radio a vessel within five or ten nautical miles — and that nothing of the kind has been heard about in this case.
  • [SINGLE SOURCE] Patterson names Michael Tenant as a former harbour master who he says was a stickler for procedure, states that Tenant resigned in January after a brief meeting with new ministers, and says he spoke to him within the last few days.
  • [SINGLE SOURCE] On flares, Patterson notes accounts that flares were seen but says nobody has established who saw them or when.
  • [SINGLE SOURCE] Patterson describes controls he says were instituted during his own tenure: an additional inspectorate layer to double-check passengers and cargo, because MARAD's technical regulatory inspection was checking only whether a load was under 100 tons. He describes ticket touting — a family of ten buying all ten tickets and reselling — as a problem the extra layer was meant to stop, and says that unit was hired directly through the Transport and Harbours Department rather than through MARAD.
  • [SINGLE SOURCE] Patterson states that under his policy a dedicated fuel barge carried fuel to Region One weekly, and that cargo for construction and mining was not permitted on the passenger vessels because space and weight were limited. He argues for separating cargo barges from passenger ferries, and for short hops from Charity or Supenaam rather than 18-to-24-hour voyages.
  • MARINE SAFETY INVESTIGATION, RELAYED. Bulkan relays MP Amanza Walton-Desir's argument that a marine safety investigation under SOLAS and the IMO is mandatory, cannot be substituted by a Commission of Inquiry, and must be independent of the state because the state is regulator, certifying authority, inspecting authority and operator at once — so it "must be capable of commanding public confidence". This corroborates the joint opposition objection already recorded in this corpus.
  • [SINGLE SOURCE] On the MSRC. Bulkan asks why information came from the Prime Minister and the Ministry of Public Works rather than from the professional staff of the Maritime Search and Rescue Coordination Centre, whose function is to coordinate rescue and report to the public. Patterson answers that the government "by default they clamp down on information" and "like to control the entire information space", and describes how a functioning centre would work: all bodies in one room, one verified figure, and a designated liaison briefing families at regular intervals. He states search and rescue was moved and coordinated under the Civil Defence Commission.
  • [SINGLE SOURCE — offers an explanation for a figure jump this archive has recorded] Patterson attributes an early jump in the survivor count to counting being split between bodies: he says survivors picked up by a different body were not counted, so the numbers moved. He offers this as an example of what a single coordinating centre would have prevented.
  • A WIDER MISSING RANGE THAN THIS ARCHIVE CARRIES. Bulkan frames the toll as 76 survivors and 73 bodies retrieved, and says of those unaccounted for "probably between 30 and 50. We still do not know how many persons are unaccounted for." Almost every other source in this corpus gives about 30. Recorded as a divergence, not adopted.
  • [ALLEGATION — relaying the account of an accused man who has not been tried] Patterson relays the captain's reported account that the vessel had no functioning anchor, no search-and-rescue transponder and no emergency position-indicating radio beacon, and that the life rafts had not been certified for four years. This archive does not adopt it.
  • CONTEXT RAISED. Bulkan refers to the Mahdia dormitory fire of three years earlier, in which she says twenty girls in the care of the state died, as a prior mass-casualty event involving indigenous people. Patterson argues against "treating the indigenous communities as byproducts".
  • NOT REACHED. Bulkan's opening agenda listed the role of ExxonMobil and Chevron in Guyana's marine space, which she said had been "largely silent", plus the treatment of Guyanese journalists, the role of fishers and self-care for medical personnel. The captured portion does not reach a substantive discussion of the oil companies.
