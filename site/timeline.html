Chronology

From the Clyde shipyard that built her in 1939 to the thirteen days after she sank. Every entry is attributed to the source that reports it; where sources conflict, both versions are given and marked.

The story in twenty-four turns

Notable developments

A reader arriving cold should be able to follow the arc before reading the record underneath. Each entry is a moment after which something was different; the full chronology, with every dated entry the corpus supports and every conflict marked, follows below.

  • 1939The Barima is builtFerguson Brothers, Port Glasgow, for the British Guiana Railways, ordered through the Crown Agents. She is the last of three sisters. The build year is contested — most coverage says 1939 and the builder’s engine register supports it; Kaieteur News says 1938, and MARAD refers to a “1938 rating”.
  • 2 Oct 2015A master mariner names this ship in printCapt. R. E. W. Adams writes to Stabroek News citing an inspection that found her deck and winch area “a mass of junk and rust”, and argues such a vessel would be barred from sea or scrapped elsewhere in the Caribbean. Eleven years before she sank.
  • 2017The state chooses rehabilitation over replacementG$150.6m is spent on her, including the installation of two engines. The 1939 hull stays.
  • 2019She becomes the only vessel on the runThe Lady Northcote is reported withdrawn, leaving an 80-year-old ship as the sole practical link between Georgetown and Port Kaituma. Contested: Kaieteur News reported on 24 July 2026 that the Lady Northcote “continues operating”.
  • Dec 2022G$1.4bn is awarded for a new Port Kaituma stellingIt reaches about 90% in February 2024, then stalls on a failed tie-rod specification and design deficiencies. It is still unfinished on the day she sinks.
  • 2023A replacement arrives and never takes the routeThe US$12.7m India-funded MV Ma Lisha is commissioned and Parliament is told it will replace the Barima. Why it never sailed the route is contested: the Prime Minister says the Port Kaituma wharf works were unfinished; residents and contemporaneous reporting say her cargo capacity was inadequate for the route. Both may be true.
  • Mar 2026Stabroek News ceases print after about forty yearsGuyana’s principal independent daily, and the paper that carried the 2015 warning, goes into voluntary liquidation four months before the sinking.
  • 9 Mar 2026A G$124.5m hull repair is put out to tenderBidding documents specify examination of some 10,000 square metres of hull plating and frames, with deteriorated sections renewed where necessary. Four months before she sinks. Whether the contract was ever awarded or the work ever done is not established in this record, and the Opposition Leader alleges it was not.
  • Sat 18 Jul 2026, 15:15She sails for Port KaitumaFrom the Transport & Harbours Department wharf at Kingston, Georgetown. The manifest records 116 passengers and 17 crew. A CCTV review will later put 179 people aboard.
  • 18 Jul, about 23:01A distress call is receivedShe capsizes off Iron Punt at the mouth of the Pomeroon River, in three to nine metres of water. Survivor accounts describe an engine repaired at sea and water entering from about 20:00.
  • 19 JulThe minister names a cause on day oneJuan Edghill: “One word… mischief.” He gives 284 tonnes licensed against 268 manifested, and discloses that only 35 of the first 67 rescued appear on the manifest. Two crew test positive for cannabis.
  • 20 JulThe loading team is suspended, and the wharf video is seizedThe suspension is announced by Facebook post. Police extract the CCTV from the Kingston Goods Wharf. The working complement becomes 179, up from the manifest’s 133.
  • 21–22 JulThe regulator clears the vesselMARAD’s Director-General states she was “not overloaded”, citing the load line. Kaieteur News publishes its own tally of 103 dead. Three days of national mourning are declared. Edghill confirms she was uninsured.
  • 24 JulThe recovery figure reaches 73 and stops73 recovered, 69 identified, about 30 unaccounted for. Boats are said to be standing by to right her. The figure of 73 will not move again.
  • 25 JulFamilies are asked to accept the wreck as a memorialA draft Declaration of Agreement is circulated asking them to concur that the vessel remain undisturbed. The same day, President Ali announces a Commission of Inquiry.
  • 26–27 JulFive commissioners are named; the divers say the wreck cannot be enteredNothing is gazetted. The French dive team tells relatives it is too dangerous to go inside — two days after the memorial document went out. Parliament sits and the opposition protests inside the chamber.
  • 28 JulThree employees of the operator are charged with murderCaptain Kevin Price, Chief Mate Rondell Roberts and Goods Superintendent Delon Granderson face 72 counts jointly and are remanded. All three are accused persons who have not been tried, and nothing on this site is a view on their guilt or innocence. No charge touches the regulator, the board or either ministry. The Prime Minister says the wreck is “likely to remain there as is”.
  • 29 JulThe captain weeps in court and the streets fillHe says the vessel was unfit to sail. Protests at Den Amstel, Melanie Damishana and the Office of the President. Edghill, on the resignation calls: “My life is in God’s hands and that’s not today.” A retired ship pilot sends this archive 253 pages on the statute book.
  • 30 JulThe Commission is sworn in, with the press shut outAt State House before the Chief Magistrate, the chairman virtually, streamed on the President’s social media instead. The enabling statute is named officially for the first time. No instrument, no published terms, no secretary, no deadline, no budget.
  • 30 JulThe government’s own release lowers the toll to 72After six days of official 73, with no announcement and no explanation, while four outlets carry 73 the same day. This archive keeps 73, and records the 72 as a divergence.
  • 30 JulA former minister of the responsible ministry treats the missing certificate as routineOn a broadcast interview, David Patterson — who held the Ministry of Public Works — treats annual certification of these vessels as routine: “how can you say you don’t have the registration certificate of a document… but you are certifying it every year.” He also says the operator has not been audited since about 2005 and argues the murder charges will silence the operator’s management as well as the accused. Uncorroborated, and captured from a machine caption track.
  • 4 AugThe inquiry finally gets its papers — and the secretary is questioned the same dayThe terms of reference are published in the Extraordinary Gazette after seventeen days: six heads including regulatory oversight by state entities, a two-month deadline, public hearings, and power to refer criminal negligence or official misconduct straight to prosecutors. A Secretary is named — and a former minister immediately objects that he holds a function in the ministry MARAD answers to. No instrument number, no budget.

The opposition attacks the timetable rather than the decision: on its account salvage cannot start before late August, while “salt water is unforgiving”.

The Opposition Leader escalates the salvage offer to a formal written request and reports no government reply of any kind.

Part I — Vessel and institutional record, 1936–2026

1936–1939. Ferguson Brothers (Port Glasgow) Ltd builds a class of three ferries for the British Guiana Railways' Transport & Harbours Department, ordered through the Crown Agents for the Colonies: Pomeroon (1936), Lady Northcote (1937) and Barima (1939). The Paxman History Pages engine register — a builder's document with no stake in the present controversy — records the Barima at 125 ft 9 in by 29 ft, twin screw, two six-cylinder Paxman MRZ engines of 180/200 bhp at £1,970 each, 8½ knots average. This is the earliest independent record of the vessel in the corpus. Kaieteur News, in a piece disputing the relevance of the vessel's age, separately dates the sister ship to 1937.

Contested Build year. Most coverage gives 1939 and describes an "87-year-old vessel," which is consistent. Kaieteur News on 25 and 26 July dates the build to 1938. The Paxman register supports 1939. The distinction matters only because MARAD's Director-General refers to a "1938 rating."

Contested Certified capacity. MARAD Director-General Capt. Stephen Thomas refers to an original 1938 rating of 120 tons and 150 passengers, and could not say when or on what basis it was raised — "I don't have that information." The figures in force in July 2026 are reported inconsistently by the two officials who cited them. Thomas gave 394 passengers / 126 tonnes on 21–22 July and 394 passengers / 120 tonnes on 24–25 July. Edghill gave 397 passengers / 284 tons on 19 July. No certificate has ever been published, so none of these can be checked.

1958–1961. Guyana builds its own ferries at Sprostons Ltd in Georgetown: Torani (keel 1958) and Malali (keel 31 May 1961). The widely syndicated claim that the Malali was British-built in 1962 is wrong.

1 August 1970. The MV Christena capsizes off St Kitts–Nevis, killing 233 — reportedly with more than 320 aboard against a certified 155. Guyanese commentators have cited it as precedent in July 2026 coverage. One source in the corpus also places her construction at the Sprostons slipway in Georgetown in 1958–59, the same yard and period as the T&HD's Torani and Malali. ⚠️ Fidelity caveat: all Christena figures and the Sprostons attribution come from a tool-extracted partial capture of a Wikipedia page (0000-00-00_wikipedia_christena-disaster.md), which the archive flags as not verbatim. Re-verify before citing.

2010. Port Kaituma residents publicly demand better vessels on the North West route.

19 September 2015. A Kaieteur News inspection report describes the Barima's deck, winch and generator area as "a mass of junk and rust." The report itself was not recoverable; the quotation survives because Capt. R. E. W. Adams quoted it.

2 October 2015. Capt. R. E. W. Adams writes to Stabroek News naming the Barima directly, citing that inspection, and arguing such a vessel would be barred from sea or scrapped elsewhere in the Caribbean. He locates the fault not in age but in missed dry-docking. It is the earliest source in the corpus to name this vessel and describe its condition directly, published eleven years before the sinking.

2017. The state elects rehabilitation over replacement, spending G$150.6m on the Barima. The project description includes "installation of two engines." Whether the 1939 machinery was therefore entirely replaced is not established in the corpus — but the hull remained original, which is the sense in which the vessel was 87 years old.

2019 Contested. The Lady Northcote is reported withdrawn, leaving the Barima as the sole vessel on the Georgetown–Port Kaituma run — the only practical link to that port, though the Kimbia and later the Ma Lisha served other Region One destinations including Kumaka. Kaieteur News on 24 July states the Lady Northcote "continues operating," which the archive flags as unresolved.

8 September 2020. DPI reports a new North West ferry "back on the agenda," recording the Barima's history of mechanical failures. ⚠️ Fidelity caveat: this file is a tool-condensed capture whose own note states that only the Edghill quotation within it is verbatim, and that quotation concerns engaging India — not the vessel's condition. The mechanical-failure characterisation should be treated as the substance of a government release, not as a quotation and not as Edghill's words.

2021. DPI, 11 June: "MV Barima to sail again after engine repairs."

2023. The US$12.7m India-funded MV Ma Lisha is commissioned, and Parliament is told it will replace the Barima and the Kimbia. It never takes the Port Kaituma route. Contested — the reasons given are incomplete Port Kaituma wharf works (Prime Minister Mark Phillips, 25 July 2026) and insufficient cargo capacity for the route's needs (residents and contemporaneous reporting). The 1939 hull stays in service.

20 September 2024. DPI: "Repairs on MV Barima to be completed soon," describing "more than 80 years of service." The vessel is docked this year; Edghill later states the next docking was due in "the latter half of 2026."

10 March 2025. DPI publishes an account of "crucial reforms" at the T&HD, conceding the department's "longstanding issues of negligence, mismanagement, and accountability." Published sixteen months before the sinking. (Verbatim; file unflagged.)

February–March 2025. Kumaka residents complain about conditions on the MV Kimbia.

October 2025. MARAD acquires two French-built Ocean Pilot boats.

19 December 2025. Demerara Waves reports the new Konawaruk's bow door will not mate with existing stellings.

13 February – 15 March 2026. Stabroek News, Guyana's principal independent daily for roughly forty years and historically the main forum for exactly the kind of technical maritime criticism Adams published in 2015, announces closure and ceases print in voluntary liquidation while still solvent. Some sixty staff go. The de Caires shareholders allege the state's Department of Public Information withheld G$84.4m in advertising arrears and that a radio licence was "repeatedly sought (and been refused)" while competitors "enjoyed significant privileges"; the paper's own editorial calls this "an assault on press freedom." Editor-in-Chief Anand Persaud told Democracy Guyana the debt "wasn't necessarily a factor," and Minister Kwame McCoy attributed the closure to market forces. Contested cause; Contested date — Stabroek reports print ceased 15 March, Kaieteur reports 14 March. Print publication ceased four months before the sinking.

9 March 2026. DPI: over G$440m to be spent on major ferry repairs. A tender is issued putting a further G$124.5m into the Barima's hull — four months before it sank. Per reporting carried by the Jamaica Observer on 25 July (unbylined CMC wire copy) and voiced by APNU MP Saiku Andrews, the bill of quantities required examination of approximately 10,000 square metres of hull plating and frames, "with deteriorated sections to be renewed where necessary." Note the conditional: this is a specification anticipating deterioration, not a survey finding that deterioration existed.

21 May 2026. Kaieteur News reports another vessel for Region One, the Kalliopi N, has arrived and is sitting uncommissioned at the Kingston Goods Wharf awaiting commissioning by "the president himself." It had still not entered service when the Barima sank — Edghill said on 22 July it "will also be put into operation soon."

21 June 2026. Dr. Karen Abrams publishes "What the oil boom owes the hinterland" in Kaieteur News — four weeks before the sinking.

Budget context. River transport figures run at G$9.9bn allocated and G$10.7bn actually spent in 2025, and G$11.2bn allocated for 2026, with nothing earmarked to retire the Barima.

Part II — The voyage, Saturday 18 July 2026

Approx. 15:15. The MV Barima departs the Transport & Harbours Department wharf at Kingston, Georgetown, bound for Port Kaituma in Region One, carrying 116 passengers and 17 crew according to the manifest. (One Ministry of Public Works rendering inside a DPI release says the vessel "departed Parika"; every other account says Kingston, Georgetown. Treat the Parika reference as an error. Contested crew: 17 in most accounts, 18 in the later official complement, 16 per Demerara Waves on 25 July.) Of the 116, 100 booked through the Online Booking System and 16 through Mobile Money Guyana.

Before departure Contested. Two passengers interviewed by Kiskadee Watch on 20 July describe trouble before the vessel left. A survivor identified as Adreiena states the engine stopped twice before departure, which she puts at about 17:00 rather than 15:15. Businessman Donald Modeste states the ferry became stuck on a sandbank at the wharf and took almost an hour to free, and that it then sailed on one engine: "when they get it off, it start working on one engine and they still go with it. They stop and had the mechanic man working on the engine and still forcing the boat to go." Minister Edghill's position is the opposite — "There was no engine problem or any mechanical problem" (19–20 July), and "there was no reported engine troubles from the crew when the boat came in and when the boat left" (22 July). Neither account has been tested against the other in any source in the corpus.

Approx. 19:00 — four hours out. One engine loses power. Survivor Leon Murray, interviewed by AP, says the crew repaired it at sea rather than turning back. Single source Janette Bulkan, writing in Kaieteur News on 25 July from survivor and journalist interviews, states the vessel became stuck on a mudbank offshore of Iron Punt, Pomeroon — a claim absent from every official account.

Approx. 20:00. A passenger reports water entering the lower deck. Murray says the crew's answer was: "They told us this is normal." Murray also describes the vessel as "packed, packed, packed all downstairs." Elena Moonsammy, who lost seven children she was travelling with, is the sole source for cargo stacked at the stern. Wayne Kitson describes a list that failed to right.

19:00–23:00. Bulkan asks whether a Pan Pan urgency call was issued during these four hours, as the SOLAS norm would require — she states Guyana acceded to SOLAS on 10 December 1997, effective 10 March 1998 (Single source, her letter). No source in the archive establishes that a Pan Pan was made.

23:01 Contested. The Barima is reported to have transmitted a Mayday by radio. BBC News gives the time as 23:01 local (03:01 GMT). Three further outlets — Deutsche Welle, CBC News and Ignite News — independently state that air traffic control received a distress call at about 23:00, which is the closest the corpus comes to identifying who took it. This time is the spine of every published timeline. On 25 July Prime Minister Phillips said "We have no such record" — of a Mayday from the captain and an instruction to passengers to don life jackets, which is a statement about the captain's conduct rather than about whether a signal reached the state. The state's own Department of Public Information had recorded the 23:01 call on 19 July. This archive previously recorded these as a flat contradiction; that was too strong and has been corrected. What remains open is that no government statement identifies who received the call, and the rescue coordination log requested on 25 July has never been produced.

Contested A second time is now on the record, and it may reconcile everything. Robert C. Millington's submitted statutory analysis of 29 July gives the distress signal as 22:43, and states that it reached an aviation authority at that time and took twenty minutes, through two intermediaries, to reach the maritime rescue authority. If that is right, 22:43 and 23:01 are both correct — the first is when the signal was received, the second when it reached the body that could act on it — and it explains why the three foreign outlets named air traffic control while the Guyanese ones named the Timehri tower. Single source to that submission, which is an analysis and not a record; the archive has not adopted it and 23:01 remains the figure used here.

The vessel is roughly seven to ten miles off the Essequibo Coast (Region Two) near Iron Punt, at the mouth of the Pomeroon River — an area known for rough seas, in water between three and nine metres deep. Several sources give "about seven miles offshore"; Demerara Waves, calculating with Google Earth Pro, gives almost ten nautical miles. She lists and does not recover. Contested sinking duration: about four minutes (Murray) versus "less than a minute" (Guyana Times).

The official proximate cause offered within days is rough seas compounded by crew impairment. MARAD's Capt. Thomas explains that a vessel with positive stability returns upright even when heeled twenty degrees, so the failure to right "points back to human operational error under treacherous conditions." Survivor accounts point instead to loading, four hours of water ingress, and an engine repaired underway.

Part III — The response, 19 July 2026

01:36. Capt. Gerry Gouveia takes off from Ogle in a Roraima Airways Islander — a fixed-wing aircraft, two and a half hours after the reported Mayday — flies twenty minutes to Iron Punt, sights a distress flare and reports GPS coordinates. Bulkan asks why the rescue helicopters and trained rescue swimmers at the Omni Helicopters Guyana base at Ogle were never launched, and why the police boat near the Pomeroon mouth and MARAD's two Ocean Pilot boats were not tasked sooner. Two GDF helicopters were reported unserviceable — raised by the opposition (via News Americas Now, 19 July) and addressed by GDF officials (via Kaieteur News, 23 July).

03:15. Edghill announces on Facebook Live that eight people have been "retrieved alive."

Through the day. Fisherman Haresh Singh locates the hull and, per Guyana Times, instructs the Coast Guard to fire flares; DPI dates the positive location of the wreck to about 13:31 on Monday 20 July, so the two accounts are sequential rather than in conflict; he mobilised his brother and other fishermen in their own boat. Single source Bulkan reports ExxonMobil sent three offshore supply vessels and ran a sonar survey, and that "Exxon or the French" also sent divers. Survivors are landed at Charity on the Essequibo Coast and assessed at Charity Hospital; bodies are processed at Charity and stored at Suddie Hospital, where four refrigerated containers are delivered. Rescued counts climb through 8, 53 and 67. President Irfaan Ali is abroad in Canada. Demerara Waves reports that, except for the GDF, the government had no crisis-communications response on the first night, and that the wreck's coordinates came from a private operator.

Part IV — Response, investigation and public reaction, 19–30 July 2026

19 July (evening). At an evening press conference Edghill stated that only 35 of the 67 rescued appeared on the manifest — meaning thirty-two survivors were never recorded as having boarded. (Contested: the Jamaica Gleaner on 20 July inverts this, reporting that at least 35 of the 67 were not on the manifest.) Edghill discloses that the captain and first engineer tested positive for cannabis; the captain, a sailor and the first engineer are held at Anna Regina, and the mate's panel differed. Edghill personally audits the cargo manifest line by line and gets an early figure of 260 tons against 268 manifested and 284 licensed. He names the cause in one word — "mischief" — and locates it in ground-level operational breach rather than regulatory failure, since the T&HD is service provider and MARAD "strictly" the regulator. DPI, meanwhile, has spent the day telling families it is working from the vessel's manifest.

20 July. Government orders an immediate investigation; the management team responsible for loading and dispatch is suspended by Facebook post, framed as protecting the integrity of the coming inquiry rather than as a finding of fault. DPI publishes its release under the headline "MV Barima was seaworthy – Min Edghill"; Edghill's own words are narrower — "There was no report of any kind that suggested the vessel was unseaworthy." Separately he states "There was no engine problem or any mechanical problem," and on 22 July that "there was no reported engine troubles from the crew when the boat came in and when the boat left." Phillips reports two crew members testing positive. Death toll moves 2 → 10 → 27, with 83 reported missing. The working passenger figure appears as 179 — up from the manifest's 133 and not derived from it — attributed to the government generally and, in CMC copy, to PM Phillips, and carried the same day by Reuters, CBC, Deutsche Welle and CMC. The National Communications Network is the named source for the 69 rescued and 83 missing, not for the 179. Ali returns and meets families.

21 July. Ali declares three days of national mourning. Toll reaches 41, then 53. Phillips restates the complement as 179 including 18 crew. Brazil and Trinidad & Tobago send assistance. APNU MP Sherod Duncan is arrested over an unlicensed drone at Charity, where he had been showing it to Warrao children. Opposition figure David Patterson alleges expert procurement advice was disregarded (post-disaster, unverified).

22 July. MARAD's Thomas states publicly the vessel was "not overloaded," citing the load line, and states that age is not determinative because modern thin steel is stronger. Kaieteur News runs a toll of 103 — its own tally compiled from three days of search work by the Coast Guard, Opposition Leader Azruddin Mohamed, private vessel owners and local fishermen; Mohamed himself said only that "it looks like over 100." The state reported 53 the same day. A third person — a T&HD superintendent responsible for Georgetown loading and ticketing — is detained. Edghill confirms the state assumed the risk on an uninsured vessel. Three women are detained at the government's national prayer service; per the activists' own subsequent statement charges were dropped and an apology given, and a constitutional action under Article 147 is weighed — no police or government statement on the incident appears in the corpus. Search area expands by 400 square miles. Jagdeo restates the toxicology findings. DPI publishes "Government response guided by transparency, compassion."

23 July. Toll 65, then 72. Al Jazeera reports around 100 feared dead — an inference from 179 minus 76 survivors — while still carrying 53 recovered, so two irreconcilable figures run side by side in international copy for roughly forty-eight hours.

24 July. PM Phillips's briefing: 73 recovered, 69 identified, 63 released to families, 30 unaccounted for, three aircraft and fourteen vessels deployed, boats standing by to right the wreck. He concedes delayed Port Kaituma wharf works are why the newer Ma Lisha could not run the route. First funerals are held; 22 by this date, "more than thirty" by 26 July. DPI announces the government has tightened ferry safety procedures — on inspection, strengthened compliance with existing standard operating procedures and stricter adherence to existing ticketing and passenger-management rules, triggered by confirmation that passengers boarded unticketed. Speaking the same day, Phillips adds that expanded CCTV coverage and improved monitoring of wharves and loading areas will form part of the reforms, alongside strengthened mandatory testing, port-clearance procedures and supervision. No manifest-verification rule, headcount protocol, life-jacket mandate, capacity cap, night-sailing ban or new inspection regime appears in any source obtained. (Jamaica Observer carried the announcement on 25 July; the DPI release and the interview are both dated 24 July. This archive previously dated the announcement to 25 July.) The National Toshaos Council urges a "comprehensive, transparent, independent review." APNU tables a Standing Order 12 motion to empower a commission to summon witnesses, compel documents and refer criminal charges.

25 July. Ali announces by video that he "shall, tomorrow – 26th of July, 2026 – announce a five-member team": an independent international Commission of Inquiry of "distinguished local and international experts." APNU and WIN reject it as unilateral. Thomas cannot explain the capacity increase from the 1938 rating. Toll holds at 73; the search area is expanded by a further 32 square miles, Waini to Adventure. APNU, through MP Saiku Andrews, demands funeral assistance of no less than G$1m per victim, against reports that some families were offered "a mere $300,000" for funeral expenses. Asked about international involvement Phillips says: "If contacting the International Maritime Organization will help in this inquiry, we will do it. We will contact all the agencies that will help in ensuring that the Guyanese people view this inquiry as an independent inquiry." Phillips's position on the wider question: "The focus now is not on compensation. The focus is on recovering bodies, financing the burial and funeral rites…" — sequencing, not a legal characterisation.

26 July. President Ali names the five-member Commission of Inquiry: Justice Godfrey Phillip Smith of Belize as chair, with Capt. Hamada Fouda (Jamaica), Nyree Dawn Alfonso (Trinidad and Tobago), Dr Andrzej Jasionowski (Poland) and Rear Admiral (Ret'd) Hayden Pritchard (Trinidad and Tobago). Terms of reference are announced covering loading, boarding and passenger conditions, the search and rescue response, compliance with maritime law and standards, negligence or institutional failure, the vessel's condition and maintenance history, weather and navigation, crew competence, manifest preparation and possible overcrowding, and reform recommendations. Single source — these names appear in the corpus only in the Guyana Chronicle's report. The establishing instruments are described as "currently being finalised," so nothing is gazetted; no statutory basis is cited by the President — the only citation in circulation is the AFC's proposal to use the Commission of Inquiry Act, Cap. 19:03 — and no secretary or reporting deadline is named. The Shipping Association of Guyana welcomes the inquiry. GHRA demands a parliamentary commission instead (Contested size: seven members per Kaieteur, six appointed equally per Demerara Waves). A joint opposition bloc of five parties makes the sharpest technical objection: a Commission of Inquiry is not a substitute for a marine casualty investigation under SOLAS, the IMO Casualty Investigation Code and the IMO Model Regulations on Domestic Ferry Safety, and both must run. Parliament has not met. As at this date the corpus contained no reference to charges being laid or an inquest being opened — in either direction. That statement describes the position on 26 July and nothing later: it was superseded on 28 July, when three Transport & Harbours Department employees were charged with murder. It is left standing here because this chronology records what was on the record when. Kaieteur reports no new developments in search and recovery, and no Saturday evening briefing. Unofficial tolls exceed 100.

26 July. The state publishes its own account of one commissioner's qualifications. The Department of Public Information profiles Captain Hamada Fouda — more than 35 years in marine regulation and casualty investigation, Senior Maritime Surveyor for Jamaica's maritime authority since 2011, leading casualty investigations under the IMO Casualty Investigation Code since 2019. President Ali is quoted saying the inquiry represents "the government's commitment to ensuring that every aspect of the tragedy is independently examined." (Added on 1 August. This archive did not hold it and had recorded DPI as publishing nothing between 25 and 30 July, which was wrong.)

27 July. The National Assembly sits. It passes a G$54.9bn supplementary budget and several bills while opposition MPs from WIN, APNU and FGM protest inside the chamber, some displaying photographs of victims, and AFC and VPAC members protest outside. Speaker Manzoor Nadir briefly suspends the sitting. The Attorney General and the Prime Minister address the House on the tragedy. The opposition demands the resignation of Ministers Edghill and Indar — the first reported inclusion of Indar. Single source APNU's Dr Terrence Campbell states that Transport & Harbours employees told the opposition they had been instructed not to speak to the press or to opposition members.

27 July. Kaieteur News and Kiskadee Watch both name the commissioners independently of the Guyana Chronicle, and Kiskadee reports the commission was appointed under Section 2(1) of the Commissions of Inquiry Act. Ali states the legal instruments "are currently being formalised"; nothing is gazetted. Kaieteur's editorial records that Speaker Manzoor Nadir has not yet ruled on APNU's Standing Order 12 motion. The French dive team tells relatives it is too dangerous to enter the wreck — ropes and hammocks inside, buried in mud, no visibility, strong tidal currents — and that lifting the vessel would be "very, very difficult", against the Prime Minister's earlier statements that boats were standing by to right it. Kako Toshao Romario Hastings and Aruau CDC Chairman Chris Debideen call for Minister Edghill's resignation in their own names. Kaieteur's "Peeping Tom" column publishes the corpus's only sustained argument against resignation. Kiskadee publishes a documented chronology of the Port Kaituma stelling, unfinished approaching four years after the G$1.4bn contract was awarded in December 2022 — the works the Prime Minister cited as the reason the Ma Lisha could not serve the route. Toll unchanged: 73 recovered, 76 rescued, at least 30 unaccounted for.

28 July. Three Transport & Harbours Department employees are charged with murder — Captain Kevin Price, 40, of Melanie Damishana, East Coast Demerara, known as "KP"; Chief Mate Rondell Dwayne Roberts, 42; and Goods Superintendent Delon Granderson, 33. The indictable charge, laid jointly on the advice of the Director of Public Prosecutions and read by Chief Magistrate Judy Latchman at the Georgetown Magistrates' Court, is murder contrary to common law. All three are remanded; the matter moves to the Charity Magistrate's Court on 3 August. Contested Roberts's address is given as "Meadow Grove Public Road" by Kaieteur News on 28 July, News Room and HGPTV, and as "Grove Public Road" by Demerara Waves and by Kaieteur News on 29 July; his forename is published as both Rondell and Rondel. All three are employees of the operator; no charge against any official, regulator or minister appears in the corpus. All three are accused persons who have not been tried, and this archive takes no view on their guilt or innocence.

The number of counts is 72, and it does not match the number of bodies. The charge is reported as 72 counts of murder in total, laid jointly against the three men — not 72 counts each. The figure is carried by Times Caribbean on 28 July and by CMC (via the Jamaica Observer), Caribbean National Weekly, WIC News and HGPTV on 29 July. Why it is 72 is explained in one place only, in defence attorney Dexter Todd's account to Caribbean National Weekly of the disclosure: "the disclosure is that 72 bodies were recovered and so they were charged for all 72 of those deaths." (The particulars themselves were published on 30 July; see that date below.) Every official figure from 24 to 29 July gives 73 bodies recovered. Both figures are recorded here and neither is adjusted to fit the other; no source in the corpus explains the difference. No Guyanese outlet reproducing the police or the DPP release states any count at all, so 72 entered circulation through court reporting and defence comment rather than through an official statement.

The Director of Public Prosecutions defends the charges. Shalimar Hack states that "The decision to do so was based upon careful consideration of the evidence presented, the relevant principles of law involved, case law authorities from the United Kingdom, Commonwealth jurisdictions, the European Court of Human Rights, and public policy considerations." On the relationship between the prosecution and the inquiry she states that "the business of this Commission of Inquiry will not affect the prosecution of the charge instituted; they can proceed simultaneously as was done in the cases of the piracy charges in the Berbice matter in 2018, and the fraud charges in the 2020 Regional and General Elections", and that "Given the unprecedented magnitude of this matter, coupled with public interest factors, the prosecution will be conducted with due consideration of the work of the Commission of Inquiry." Demerara Waves (Denis Chabrol) records that she spoke "without directly addressing the legal elements — intent and malice — of murder", and HGPTV likewise records that the release did not explain how the elements of murder would be established. Single source Demerara Waves' lead characterises the DPP as having stated that the vessel's navigational and safety systems were defective. No direct quotation of her saying this appears in any source; iNews reproduces the same release at near-full length without it, and HGPTV omits it. It is recorded here as that outlet's characterisation and not as a statement or admission by the DPP. An earlier version of this page carried it as a DPP statement; that was an error and is corrected here.

Defence attorneys Nigel Hughes and Darren Wade raise concerns about the charges. Hughes argues that murder charges could silence key witnesses before the Commission of Inquiry, because the accused may stay silent at the inquiry to avoid self-incrimination. Todd calls them "holding charges" and states that the police acted before the investigation was complete.

The same day the Prime Minister states the vessel is "likely to remain there as is", that no one has been contacted to raise it, that attempts to move it onto its keel have failed, and that "it's obvious that bodies are trapped in the wreck." Demerara Waves reports the government willing to leave the vessel on the sea floor even if it holds evidence useful to the inquiry. Kaieteur reports a draft "Declaration of Agreement", circulated to families on 25 July, asking them to accept the wreck as an undisturbed "sacred memorial" with a monument bearing the victims' names. Contested characterisation of that instrument: this archive records it, following Kaieteur News, as a draft "Declaration of Agreement"; Chabrol, in Demerara Waves, describes the same document circulated on 25 July as a questionnaire asking families to choose between salvaging the vessel and leaving it in place as a memorial. One document, two materially different descriptions; both are given and neither is merged into the other. Relatives allege this may abandon salvage prematurely and obscure physical evidence before the Commission of Inquiry can inspect the hull. The report sets this against President Ali's assurance of 20 July that the effort would continue "until every possible avenue has been exhausted." The French team states the wreck "is not very strong".

Christopher Ram argues that the Commission of Inquiry "dodges key issues" and that it should have been constituted under Part XVIII of the Shipping Act. That is the third statute named in public as the proper basis, alongside the AFC's proposal of the Commission of Inquiry Act, Cap. 19:03 and Kiskadee Watch's report of section 2(1) of the Commissions of Inquiry Act. On 30 July the government stated the basis itself — Cap. 19:03, s. 2(1) — so Ram's letter now reads as an objection to a choice that has been made rather than as an open question about which statute was used. Nothing has been gazetted, so the instrument itself still cannot be checked.

28 July. The Guyana Defence Force says an after-action review of the rescue will be conducted, and describes exactly what it will read. Colonel Kenlloyd Roberts, Chief of the General Staff, announces a review to begin within a day or two of the search concluding, covering communications and transmissions between the control tower, the lighthouse, the Search and Rescue Coordination Centre and Coast Guard operations, together with official reports, from the initial distress signal to the end of the mission — as this archive's condensed capture of the release renders it; the release is not held verbatim. "This operation will not be completed from our side until that activity takes place." No deadline, no named recipient and no undertaking to publish appear, and the release does not say whether it will go to the Commission of Inquiry. (Added on 1 August; this archive did not hold it.)

28 July. Two state releases the same day give the figures and the conditions. The Prime Minister gives 73 recovered, 68 identified and released, five awaiting identification, 30 unaccounted for, 76 survivors of 77 rescued, with 18 assets deployed — three aircraft and 15 vessels — from Waini Point to Adventure: "Every effort continues to be made to complete the identification process with the utmost care and dignity." Separately, Lieutenant (N) Tyler Ferraz of the Trinidad and Tobago Coast Guard describes conditions at the wreck — "a lot of the divers are unable to see their hands in front of their face, anything [not] more than a foot" — against a muddy seabed, strong tidal currents and shallow water. Contested That release gives 17 primary assets — 14 vessels and three aircraft — against the Prime Minister's 18 the same day, both from the state. Neither carries a time, so which came first is not established. (Both added on 1 August.)

29 July. The Amerindian Peoples Association pickets the Office of the President, demanding accountability for the disaster and the removal of Minister of Public Works Juan Edghill and Minister of Public Utilities and Aviation Deodat Indar. It first reached this archive through two social-media accounts — Sheriff News Network, describing a peaceful picket by APA members and supporters, and Prime News Guyana, reporting that "many Guyanese joined" — and an earlier build of this page recorded that no press report of it appeared in the corpus. That is no longer true and the note is withdrawn: the picket is carried by Kaieteur News, News Room, Kiskadee Watch and Demerara Waves, retrieved on 30 July. Crowd size is given as "roughly 100" by Kaieteur and "over a hundred" by Kiskadee. Placards recorded include "Indigenous Lives Matter", "Edghill and Indar Must Go", "Stop treating our first people as an afterthought" and "We deserve safe passage home". APA Executive Director Jean La Rose: "Whether it is by air, we have leaking planes, whether it is by boat we have leaking boats, whether it is by roads, we have poor roads," and, on the wreck, "It carries a lot of information. It carries a lot of evidence. It carries souls of the people. It must be raised so our people can have closure." The APA's statement is that "The sinking of the MV Barima was not an unavoidable accident." Single source Protester Cowen Gittens is reported by Kiskadee arguing that the government bore responsibility as vessel owner, operator and sector regulator at once; and Andrew Campbell called for the resignation of a third minister, Minister of Amerindian Affairs Sarah Browne, alongside Edghill and Indar. The APA's written call for Edghill's removal was already on the record from 23 July; this is an escalation to public assembly, and it now names Indar as well.

29 July. Minister Edghill responds to the resignation demands for the first time, and does not engage the substance: asked about the calls he says he has "repeatedly said I will speak about this matter at the appropriate time"; asked about the protests, "My life is in God's hands and that's not today." The demand now comes from five opposition parties — WIN, APNU, FGM, V-PAC and AFC — and covers both ministers. MP Ganesh Mahipaul's stated grounds are the reports of overloading, inadequate systems aboard the vessel, and the Port Kaituma stelling contract awarded in 2022 and still incomplete. Prime Minister Phillips defends both ministers remaining in office and calls the opposition's parliamentary protest "irresponsible, reckless, childish, immature", asserting the inquiry is independent and its members internationally selected. (Kaieteur News.)

29 July. Kaieteur News reports the captain's appearance in court. Kevin Price wept, said the vessel was unfit to sail, and said "we don't deserve this." That is a record of what he is reported to have said in court and not a finding about the vessel's condition; he and his two co-accused, Rondell Dwayne Roberts and Delon Granderson, are accused persons who have not been tried, and this archive takes no view on their guilt or innocence.

The wreck: the government's stated position moves within twenty-four hours. Opposition Leader Azruddin Mohamed publicly offers to raise the vessel — "give me permission and I will raise it." Having said on 28 July that the wreck was "likely to remain there as is" and that no one had been contacted to raise it, the government now acknowledges that multiple salvage proposals exist and states that the families will decide. Prime Minister Phillips is reported to concede that international salvage companies "were prepared to raise" the vessel. The two positions are twenty-four hours apart; both are recorded, and neither is treated here as superseding the other on the record.

Phillips moves off "not overloaded." Asked whether the vessel was overloaded, the Prime Minister now refers the question to the Commission of Inquiry rather than answering it. MARAD's Director-General Capt. Stephen Thomas stated categorically on 22 July, citing the load line, that the vessel was "not overloaded." The regulator's finding and the head of government's referral stand side by side; no source in the corpus reconciles them.

Single source Former captain Andrew Donald makes a series of claims about the vessel and the department, published by HGPTV on 29 July. They originate in a Facebook post of the same day by MP Ganesh Mahipaul carrying Donald's statement — a social-media source, not a document of record. Donald states that the Barima was a single-hull passenger vessel operating contrary to SOLAS, with no hull tanks to contain water ingress and no operational bilge system adequate to serious flooding, leaving five to ten minutes to respond to a breach; that "The Barima was not supposed to be in service"; that "Every voyage was a never-ending war with management"; that captains were pressured by wharf staff, business owners and T&HD managers over cargo and departure times, that bribes were attempted and that transfer, suspension or dismissal were threatened; that "I blame T&HD and its subject ministers"; and that the three accused are scapegoats. HGPTV states that the claims are not independently verified and that Donald named no individuals. Until 30 July no other outlet in the corpus carried him; Kiskadee Watch has since published the same account at greater length, which corroborates the report without supplying a second witness, since both rest on Donald. The image attached to the Facebook post carries Meta's own "AI-generated content" label and is not a photograph.

Figures. 73 bodies recovered, of which 68 identified and five unidentified (Kaieteur News and iNews, 28–29 July; WIC News gives 68 identified). This supersedes the 69 identified reported by the Prime Minister on 24 July. Phillips states that 77 people were rescued and 76 survived, one having died after rescue — which resolves the 76/77 survivor discrepancy this archive had carried unresolved. The complement of 179 and the approximately 30 unaccounted for are unchanged.

29 July. The Attorney General defends the murder charges personally and characterises the accused. [ALLEGATION] Anil Nandlall is reported by News Source Guyana to have said that "accounts indicate the captain was under the influence of alcohol and narcotics," to have identified the three charged men as "primary offenders" and "principal offenders," and to have said passengers were not wearing life jackets, "something the captain should have ensured." These are statements by a member of the Executive about named accused persons who have not been tried, made while the matter is before the court. They are recorded here as a fact about what the Attorney General said and as an unproven allegation, and nothing more. On the opposition he said: "The Opposition has ceased [seized] this moment to exploit it for their narrow political gains," describing the National Assembly protest as "lawless and vulgar"; iNews carries him on 30 July saying opposition legislators "have exploited people's anger, they have exploited people's trauma, they have exploited people's pain."

29 July. A belief attributed to the Prime Minister, which this archive cannot locate him expressing. [SINGLE SOURCE, and qualified] Demerara Waves reports, on 29 and again on 30 July, that the three accused face murder charges "despite Prime Minister Mark Phillips stating he did not believe 'they set out to kill occupants'" and "did not believe there was a deliberate effort to kill anyone." Both are back-references, the second in the pluperfect; neither is dated, neither quotes an occasion, and no press conference, transcript, release or rival outlet anywhere in this corpus records him saying it. It is recorded here as what that outlet reported and not as an established statement by the head of government — a distinction this archive has already had to draw once about a framing sentence from the same outlet on this same story, on 28 July, concerning the Director of Public Prosecutions. An earlier version of this entry, and a draft finding on the analysis page, treated it as established; both were withdrawn on 30 July. Note also that on one Guyanese legal commentary in this corpus, "he did not set out to kill" would not in any event answer the charge, because extreme recklessness may supply the malice the law requires.

29 July. The legal profession divides publicly over the charges. Kaieteur News compiles the reaction. Defence attorney Dexter Todd: "The police have done what the commission of inquiry is supposed to do and they have already come to a definitive conclusion on this matter"; he has requested early disclosure of the prosecution's evidence, and Single source raises sub judice as a limit on the commission — "if a matter is before one court, then not even a commission can look into the affairs of that matter." Darren Wade: "these murder charges are legally nonsensical," and, on scope, "Is the institution of murder charges having the effect of shielding senior officials from closer scrutiny by focusing public attention almost exclusively on the Captain and crew?" Single source Former Assistant Commissioner of Police Paul Slowe: "It begs the question: what is the position with the ministers with oversight responsibilities?" [ALLEGATION] AFC interim leader David Patterson infers what the prosecution must hold: "For the Director of Public Prosecutions to recommend a murder charge, she must have received information that strongly suggests the goods superintendent deliberately overloaded the vessel" — his inference about the case, not a finding. APNU's Dr Terrence Campbell calls the charges "scapegoating".

29 July. The Ministry of Public Works publishes an itemised list of its own cargo aboard. Single source Kiskadee Watch, reporting on 30 July, carries the Ministry's statement of "the consigned cargo aboard the MV Barima, on the Ministry's behalf, at the time of the July 18 Departure": one Honda side-by-side ATV, six drums of gasoline and four drums of diesel. It is a statement by the Ministry about its own consignment only. It is not a manifest, it is not audited, it says nothing about total cargo aboard or about stowage, and it should not be read as a finding on loading.

29–30 July. Protests spread to three sites. Family members and Transport & Harbours Department staff demonstrate at Den Amstel on the West Coast Demerara, at Melanie Damishana on the East Coast, and outside the Office of the President, all on Wednesday 29 July. About thirty gathered at Den Amstel, chanting "jail the criminals, free the innocent" and carrying placards reading "wrongly accused, wrongly judged" and "free Delon Granderson". Demerara Waves, HGPTV and Kiskadee Watch all place the protests on Wednesday; Kaieteur News's lead says "Thursday morning and evening" and its own fifth paragraph then refers to "Wednesday's protests" — an internal contradiction in that report, resolved here against it. Opposition Leader Azruddin Mohamed is reported announcing plans for demonstrations across all ten administrative regions. HGPTV confirms that the accused remain on remand and that the Charity Magistrate's Court date of 3 August is unchanged.

30 July. The five commissioners are sworn in, twelve days after the sinking. Four take the oath in person at State House, Georgetown, before Chief Magistrate Judy Latchman; chairman Justice Godfrey Phillip Smith, not yet in Guyana, is sworn virtually. President Ali attends. Five sources in this corpus carry it: DPI, Demerara Waves, News Room, News Source Guyana and Kiskadee Watch. (iNews Guyana published a sixth account the same day. It is not held here, because the sweep that found it could not retrieve the article text, so it is not counted.) The press was not invited — Demerara Waves and News Source Guyana both state so, and News Source records that the ceremony was streamed on the President's social media accounts instead. DPI states the enabling statute for the first time in the government's own words: section 2(1) of the Commissions of Inquiry Act, Cap. 19:03. Ali: the commission "meets the highest benchmarks of independence, impartiality, and objectivity," and "every resource required" will be made available. No sum is given. Single source Demerara Waves: "The Secretary to the commission has not yet been named." Single source Kiskadee Watch reports Ali saying the commissioners will determine their own procedures — so no rules of procedure existed on the day they were sworn. Nothing is gazetted; no terms of reference are published as a document, no reporting deadline, no budget and no call for submissions.

30 July. The official death toll moves backwards, from 73 to 72, with no announcement. DPI's own release and Demerara Waves both state that "72 bodies were recovered, 30 could not be accounted for and 76 rescued" out of an estimated 179. Every official figure since 24 July had given 73. On the same day News Room, Kaieteur News (three separate reports), Kiskadee Watch and iNews Guyana all still carry 73. No source announces a revision or explains the change. This archive continues to carry 73 as the figure of record, because it is the one every official source gave from 24 to 29 July, and records the 72 as an unexplained divergence rather than a correction. The likeliest mechanism, and it is inference not record, is contamination from the 72 counts on the charge sheet.

30 July. The charge particulars are published for the first time. Kaieteur News reports that the indictable charge alleges that on 18 July 2026, at a point 9.3 nautical miles on a bearing of 040° off the Essequibo Coast at Devonshire Castle, the three men murdered 72 named victims. That is the first account in the corpus of how the number 72 is constituted: one count for each named person. Granderson's address is given as Fellowship, West Coast Demerara — new to this record.

30 July. Legal representation and compensation reach the record for the first time. Opposition Leader Azruddin Mohamed announces he will assemble a legal team for the families and tells them not to sign anything: "I told them do not sign any paperwork," and "I will have a legal team to represent them to ensure that they are handsomely compensated." He pledges to pursue the matter at the Caribbean Court of Justice if necessary, and reports that most families he consulted oppose memorialisation and want the remains recovered. Separately, a Kaieteur letter argues that families should be taking independent legal advice on civil claims now rather than waiting for the commission to report. A named relative gives a payment figure: Louvicia Poon of Wakenaam, who lost her eleven-year-old son Ronaldo and her seventeen-year-old brother Christian Rodrigues, says she received $350,000 from the government on 28 July for funeral expenses — "I had to accept what I got." The archive already carries APNU's complaint of 25 July that some families were offered "a mere $300,000" and its demand for no less than G$1m. These are two different figures from two different kinds of source, and neither is a statement of policy; both stand.

30 July. Two assessments of the state's care of the bereaved are published on the same day and they do not agree. PAHO's Country Representative in Guyana, Dr Kim Eva Dickson, praises the response after a visit to Region One — "We were really impressed with the efforts on the ground" — but she says it at the signing of an unrelated One Health agreement, not at a Barima event and not as the product of any assessment mission. Single source Clinical psychotherapist Dr Shane Tull, on HGPTV, calls the response "an improvised response rather than a structured national emergency plan," criticises the use of an open public venue, the Umana Yana, as the primary centre for grieving families — "You cannot place families dealing with catastrophic grief in an open public space… and expect that to suffice" — and says services must run "not just for the next two weeks, but for the next two years," extending to the fishermen, divers and volunteers who recovered bodies. Both are recorded, and neither is treated as settling the other.

30 July. Chief Engineer Paul Seepersaud, 44, is laid to rest at the Wesleyan Church, New Amsterdam, after a funeral on 29 July; he had more than three decades at sea with the Transport & Harbours Department. Single source His sister told Kaieteur News that she identified him by the tattoos on his body — the only concrete account in the corpus of how any identification was made. Kaieteur also gives a regional tally new to this record: four Berbice victims recovered and buried — Orin Anthony Clarke, 54, of New Amsterdam; Kevin Wilson, 28, of Glasgow Village; Devon Mars, 46, of Sandvoort Village; and Mark Abrams, 54, of No. 53 Village, Corentyne — with Raymond Somra, 56, of Islington Village still unaccounted for.

30 July. Civil society challenges the commission on the day it is sworn. Single source TIGI president Mike Singh, on HGPTV, questions the neutrality of several appointees and calls on the President to reopen the framework of the commission to civil society, religious bodies and victims' families. The APA's Jean La Rose states the association was not consulted on the terms of reference or the selection of commissioners. The parliamentary opposition welcomes the commission but raises the same lack of consultation and "potential conflicts of interest" (Single source — News Source Guyana). Kiskadee Watch's editorial of the same day puts the resignation question as a constitutional one: "neither Minister nor the President has responded in any meaningful way," and "Silence, when repeated often enough, becomes policy."

30 July. A former Minister of Public Works speaks at length, and treats the missing certificate as routine. On OGGN Oil Talk, streamed live and hosted by Janette Bulkan, David Patterson — AFC shadow minister for the portfolio and a former holder of it — gives the first extended account in this corpus by anyone who has run the responsible ministry. Single source throughout, and captured from a machine-generated caption track rather than a publisher's transcript. He treats annual certification of these vessels as routine — "how can you say you don't have the registration certificate of a document… but you are certifying it every year" — which goes to the document this archive has recorded as never produced since day one. He states the Transport and Harbours Department has not turned a profit since "2007 or 2009" and has not been officially audited since "most likely I think 2005, 2006." He cites Cap. 49:07 s. 18 as having given inspectors power to detain unfit vessels and prevent overloaded ones from sailing. He argues the murder charges will hollow out the Commission beyond the three accused, because the marine superintendent, the chief engineer and "the general manager of transport and harbours" will not incriminate themselves either, leaving "circumstantial" and "he say, she say" evidence — and predicts masters may refuse to sail. Asked why briefings came from ministers rather than the Maritime Search and Rescue Coordination Centre's professional staff, he answers that the government "by default they clamp down on information." Bulkan, for her part, puts the rescue gap in one line from the Prime Minister's own account: the first rescue "took place at 27 minutes past 2 on Sunday, that's more than 3 hours later." Apart from the annual-survey convention Capt. Adams described in 2015, which runs with Patterson's certification account, none of it is corroborated elsewhere in this corpus.

30 July. A second outlet carries the former master, at greater length. [ALLEGATION] Kiskadee Watch publishes Andrew Donald's account a day after HGPTV: twelve years at the Transport and Harbours Department, the vessel "a floating disaster waiting to occur", and allegations of chronic overloading, inadequate watertight subdivision, bilge systems unable to control flooding, breaches of SOLAS, a single-hull design he considers unfit for passengers, and delayed dry-docking. On who controlled loading: "They have the power to load, overload and set sailing time for the vessels. But when something goes wrong, they all back-pedalled and left the captain in all the mess." This corroborates HGPTV's report; it is not a second witness, since both rest on Donald. He names no official and nothing in it is independently verified. This archive adopts none of it. It plainly bears on the charged men — it is an argument that others controlled loading and that masters were left carrying the consequence — and it is recorded as one man's untested allegation, not as fact, for exactly that reason. (Added 2 August.)

31 July. Defence counsel moves to stop the two proceedings running together. Attorney Dexter Todd, who acts for all three accused and is also an APNU Member of Parliament, tells a press conference he is preparing a court challenge to the Director of Public Prosecutions' position that the prosecution and the Commission may proceed simultaneously. He puts the jurisdictional question as: "What will stand down? Will the COI stand down or will the criminal court stand down?" He argues the casualty belongs under the Guyana Shipping Act rather than common-law murder. Single source He makes his clients' participation in the Commission conditional on three things — whether the criminal proceedings are stayed, whether adequate legal safeguards exist, and whether the terms of reference are made available for review. This is the first time in the corpus that the accused's participation in the inquiry is stated to be conditional on anything.

31 July. Seaworthiness is put squarely on the regulator. Todd: "None of the parties charged are to determine seaworthiness of that vessel. Seaworthiness of the vessel carries a particular procedure, and that has to be determined by, of course, MARAD." [ALLEGATION] He relays Captain Price's account that the vessel had no radar, no EPIRB, no search-and-rescue transponder and no functioning anchor, and Price's statement that "Those life rafts expired since 2022, and Maritime did an inspection and did not write off that vessel." These are the untested assertions of a man awaiting trial, relayed by his own counsel, and this archive adopts none of them. MARAD has said nothing publicly since 24 July and answers none of it.

31 July. The Opposition Leader escalates the salvage question from offer to formal request, and reports that the government has not replied to him at all — "No update, nothing at all." Single source Kiskadee Watch reports that the government "circulated documents requesting relatives consent to designating the vessel a memorial site", which if accurate moves the memorial proposal from a draft discussed with families to paperwork put in front of them. Mohamed also states that no family should be pressured into signing anything or into accepting "any meagre sum of money." Against the memorial proposal, the Assakata Village Council in Region One formally resolves that the vessel should be raised to allow a complete and credible investigation — the first recorded resolution by an Indigenous village council on the question.

31 July. The resignation campaign reaches the minister's gate. A joint opposition vigil is held for about two hours outside Minister Juan Edghill's home in South Ruimveldt, with police present; Azruddin Mohamed, Dr Terrence Campbell and MPs Primus, Panday and Duncan attend. Mohamed's formulation is that both Edghill and Minister Indar "should be sent home pending the outcome of this inquiry." No resignation follows and no government response to the vigil appears in this corpus. Separately, Kaieteur News records that President Ali has not faced the local media since returning to the country after the disaster, having communicated by video statement while the Prime Minister fronted the briefings.

31 July. New detail on why the Port Kaituma wharf failed. APNU MP Ganesh Mahipaul attributes the failure of the G$1.4bn works to pre-construction design and site investigation: he names CEMCO as the designer, and describes piles that never reached firm ground, siting on a former "Log Pond" sawmill site with buried debris, an oversized retaining wall, undersized steel, underestimated backfill, and tie rods that "snapped under the pressure." Single source, and it is an opposition MP's account rather than an engineering report. It bears on the chain the Prime Minister has already conceded: the unfinished wharf is why the Ma Lisha could not take the route, which is why an 87-year-old vessel was still running it.

31 July. A framework for the assistance scheme is begun, and it is again described as not compensation. Guyana Times reports that a team has been assembled to draft the national framework for the HEART initiative, and states that the assistance "is not intended as compensation." No members are named, no sum is given and no timetable is set. Nothing on 31 July states a compensation scheme, a quantum, or any admission of liability.

31 July. Questions about two commissioners are put and not answered. Single source Kiskadee Watch reports that it put conflict-of-interest questions concerning Commissioners Capt. Hamada Fouda and Nyree Dawn Alfonso to AAMOTI, the President's Press Unit and Minister Keoma Griffith, and received no response. The Opposition Leader states doubts about the commissioners. On the other side, the Guyana Congregational Union publicly hopes the Commission will begin its work "very soon." No first sitting, gazetted instrument, published terms of reference, secretary, deadline or budget appears in any document dated 31 July.

31 July. On the toll, the divergence is not resolved — it goes quiet. Every Guyanese outlet putting a number to it on 31 July uses 73 dead, about 30 missing, 76 rescued: Kaieteur News in three separate reports and Guyana Times in its Commission report. The two outlets that carried 72 on 30 July — DPI and Demerara Waves — publish nothing on 31 July and neither defend nor correct it. Note that Guyana Times, the outlet closest to the administration, now carries 73 while the government's own information service still carries 72, and nobody acknowledges the gap. The regional wires meanwhile abandon precision altogether, giving "more than 70" or no figure at all, and two letter-writers treat the combined dead-and-missing total as a confirmed death count of 100. No new bodies are reported recovered.

31 July. The recovery phase ends, and the President is reported to have committed to a lift. Single source iNews Guyana reports the Prime Minister confirming that diving has ceased and that the French Guiana Army, Trinidad and Tobago Coast Guard and Brazilian Navy teams have left the country; the 18-asset operation is scaled back to patrol boats and daily aerial surveillance. About thirty people were never recovered. Separately, and more consequentially, relatives of three missing Berbicians — Raymond Sumra, Rawle Frank and Antoine "Tony" Harcourt — say that at a meeting on 29 July President Ali committed to attempting to salvage the vessel, and that they were told a lift could take eight to ten weeks. Puran Grant, Sumra's uncle: "What he had told me [is] he [will] try his best to bring it up." No government release in this corpus confirms either the commitment or the timeframe, and the Prime Minister the same day restates the position that the government is still weighing salvage against a memorial and will be guided by the families. Both are recorded; neither supersedes the other. (Both added on 1 August.)

1 August (announced 31 July). A second investigative body is announced, and it is not the Commission. President Ali establishes a Safety and Compliance Audit Team to review the safety and compliance standards governing marine vessels in Guyana: Dr Ivor English, a former Director-General of MARAD, as leader, with Colonel Julius Skeete of the GDF and Captain Joseph Lewis, Marine Officer and Surveyor of Ships. Ali: the team will "examine compliance with applicable maritime safety requirements and make recommendations to strengthen oversight, improve enforcement and ensure that our vessels, operators and regulatory systems meet the highest practicable standards of safety and accountability." Carried by Kaieteur News and Guyana Times, so not single-source. It arrives, like the Commission before it, with no deadline, no budget, no terms of reference published as a document and no undertaking to publish its findings, and nothing states how it relates to the Commission of Inquiry. Its remit is prospective and general rather than directed at the Barima.

1 August (spoken 31 July). The opposition leader puts the charging decision in the sharpest terms yet. Single source Aubrey Norton, PNCR chairman and APNU leader, at the party's weekly press conference on Friday 31 July, calls the murder charges "ridiculous, vindictive, and intended to silence the workers" and says nothing publicly shown "proves that these men deliberately intended to kill passengers". These are an opposition leader's assertions about a live prosecution and this archive adopts none of them; the three men are charged and untried. He moves the question upward — "Who allowed a government vessel with defective systems to sail? Who certified it? Who supervised its maintenance?" — demands publication of the cargo manifests, weight certificates, maintenance reports, surveillance recordings and inspection certificates, and adds a demand new to this corpus: that the state say which ministries owned the cargo aboard. He repeats the call for Ministers Edghill and Indar to resign.

1 August. The first published defence of running the inquiry and the prosecution together. Kaieteur News's "Peeping Tom" column argues the two serve different purposes — systemic fact-finding against individual guilt — and that both can proceed. Single source Its substantive claim, which no one in government has made, is that the Commissions of Inquiry Act already contains provisions protecting witnesses from self-incrimination, and that Parliament therefore contemplated the overlap. This archive holds no statutory text against which to check it. The column cites the South Korean MV Sewol disaster of 2014 as precedent for parallel proceedings, and carries the newspaper's standing disclaimer that the views are the author's.

1 August. The state moves to raise the ship, through a tender notice rather than an announcement. MARAD, under the Ministry of Public Utilities and Aviation, issues an Invitation for Expressions of Interest for marine salvage contractors to recover the vessel, closing 14 August. The stated purpose is evidential: bidders must set out a technical approach protecting "forensic evidence for the ongoing Commission of Inquiry", and News Room reports the evidence "is expected to assist the international Commission of Inquiry established to determine what caused the vessel to capsize." The Prime Minister had already conceded on 28 July that the wreck "likely contains additional remains and investigative evidence" while saying the state was willing to leave it there; what is new is that the state is now paying to recover it. The notice gives the wreck's position as 07° 28′.213 N, 058° 23′.443 W in 13–15 metres of water with "strong currents and poor visibility", the vessel as 38.6 m by 8.9 m by 2.9 m with twin Caterpillar C18 diesel engines, and requires bidders to accept "no cure, no pay" terms and to hold the vessel on a barge for weeks. MARAD reserves the right to shortlist for a full Request for Proposals or to cancel the process entirely. Contested The 38.6 m length does not agree with the 40.26 m carried elsewhere in this corpus; The 13–15 m is not in conflict with the three-to-nine-metre range reported in the first week: that range was given for the Iron Punt area generally, "depending on the vessel's distance from the shoreline", and reconciles itself.

2 August. The memorial plan is gone, and nobody in government has said so. Kaieteur News reports that the procurement notice is also the abandonment of the plan to designate the site a memorial — the plan that went to bereaved families as a draft declaration on 25 July. Single source The reason it gives is "mounting pressure from stakeholders across Guyana", with families rejecting the memorial and demanding recovery of remains. No government statement in this corpus acknowledges the change of position, gives a reason for it, or mentions the memorial proposal at all. A proposal put to the bereaved in writing on 25 July was dropped through a tender notice seven days later.

2 August. The opposition attacks the salvage decision on speed, not on principle. APNU MP Ganesh Mahipaul argues the expression-of-interest route pushes the start of salvage to late August or early September once evaluation, shortlisting, bidding, contracting and mobilisation are counted: "That timeline is simply unacceptable." He calls for emergency procurement instead, and warns that the evidence the notice exists to protect is deteriorating meanwhile — "Salt water is unforgiving. Mechanical systems corrode, electronic equipment deteriorates…" WIN's position is that the delay leaves families awaiting "answers and closure they deserve", and Mahipaul criticises the handling of the Opposition Leader's offer to fund a salvage privately — which this archive records as unanswered rather than rejected, the Opposition Leader having said on 31 July that he had had "no update, nothing at all". No government response appears. Contested The report carries figure errors against every other source here — it dates the sinking to 19 July and inverts the survivor and fatality counts — recorded as the outlet's, not as movement in the record.

3 August. The Charity Magistrate's Court date recorded in this archive passes, and nothing in the corpus records what happened. The three accused were remanded on 28 July to appear on 3 August. No document retrieved on 3 or 4 August reports that hearing — no adjournment, no bail application, no amended charge, no additional accused. Recorded as an absence in this archive's retrieval, not as an absence of proceedings.

3 August. A second statutory analysis is submitted to this archive, from the author of the first. Fifty-two pages, published here in full, widening the scope from maritime law to sea and river, road, rail and air, with a consolidated schedule of 52 deficiencies across the four modes, 31 of them marked Critical. Single source One of its positions cuts against what this site had published from his first document five days earlier: he places the vessel inside Part X of the Guyana Shipping Act, on the ground that a mechanically propelled steel passenger ferry is none of the six exceptions in s. 297 — where his first analysis turned on a 24-metre band this document never mentions. He does not describe it as a correction and does not refer to the earlier submission. He also sets out Cap. 49:03 s. 7(1), requiring the master to declare in writing the number, names, sex and age of every person on board before clearance, with a year's imprisonment for a false declaration. This archive briefly read that as overturning his earlier finding that no counting duty exists, and was wrong to: the same document repeats that the s. 398(1)(b) power was never exercised and still proposes creating a duty of passenger accounting, so the two provisions describe different things — a customs pre-clearance paper against a headcount kept ashore. The withdrawal was itself withdrawn; see the revisions page. Also new: an annual survey duty in terms (s. 257, "a periodic survey at intervals of not more than twelve months"); a Certificate of Seaworthiness regime under the Harbours Regulations 1939 from which reg. 6(2)(a) exempts "vessels belonging to the State", five words he says could be deleted by regulation without any parliamentary step; a second route into survey through the Tax Act vessel licence, which contains no State exemption; and a warning that the 2022 Revised Edition of the Laws of Guyana was presented to the National Assembly on 19 June 2026, twenty-nine days before the loss, while his analysis rests on the 2012 edition the Ministry still publishes.

4 August. The Commission's terms of reference are published, and two of this archive's longest-standing gaps close in one day. The President publishes the terms in the Extraordinary Gazette. Six heads: vessel seaworthiness and safety; passenger and cargo operations; crew qualifications and conduct; search and rescue response effectiveness; regulatory oversight by state entities; and human factors in emergency decision-making. A two-month deadline is set for final written findings — the first deadline of any kind in this record. Hearings are public with discretionary closed sessions, commissioners may issue interim safety reports, and the panel has explicit authority to refer evidence of criminal negligence, official misconduct or statutory breaches directly to prosecuting authorities. The five commissioners are listed again, unchanged from 26 July: chair Godfrey Smith, Capt. Hamada Fouda, Rear Admiral Hayden Pritchard, Nyree Dawn Alfonso and Dr Andrzej Jasionowski. Still absent: a Statutory Instrument or Gazette number, and any budget figure.

4 August. A secretary is named, and his independence is immediately questioned. Attorney Javed Shadick is Secretary of the Commission — closing a gap Demerara Waves recorded as open on 30 July. Single source David Patterson objects on three grounds: Shadick is the son of a former PPP minister and GECOM Commissioner; he is currently Secretary of the Constitution Reform Commission; and he holds a function in the Guyana Civil Aviation Authority, which sits under the Ministry of Public Utilities and Aviation. The next step is this archive's and not Patterson's: that is the ministry MARAD answers to — MARAD's own salvage notice of 1 August says so — and MARAD's regulatory oversight is one of the six heads the Commission has just been empowered to investigate. Patterson: "out of the thousands of available lawyers, he has been selected once again…" He also puts a timing objection that is checkable: "The commissioners acknowledge that salvaging the vessel is necessary so the wreck can be examined for evidential purposes. Yet, despite this, they have limited the inquiry to just two months." The salvage tender closes 14 August; two months from 4 August is about 4 October. No government response appears, and this is the second set of conflict-of-interest questions about this Commission to go unanswered in five days.

Figures reported, and the arithmetic they imply

No official death toll appears in the corpus. Government releases report bodies recovered. Against a stated complement of 179 and 76–77 survivors, subtraction leaves 102–103 people. On 29 July the Prime Minister gave 77 rescued and 76 surviving, one having died after rescue, which resolves that range: the subtraction then leaves 103. Seventy-three bodies had been recovered as of 26 July, and the figure was unchanged on 29 July; 68 of the 73 are identified and five are not. The difference — approximately thirty people — does not appear as a figure in any government release in this corpus.

On 30 July the recovery figure moved backwards in the government's own release. DPI and Demerara Waves gave 72 bodies recovered; News Room, Kaieteur News, Kiskadee Watch and iNews Guyana gave 73. No source announced a revision and none explains the change. This archive keeps 73 as the figure of record, on the ground that it is what every official source gave from 24 to 29 July, and records 72 as an unexplained divergence rather than a correction. Note what the divergence does to the arithmetic: on 72, the residual against 179 and 76 survivors is 31 rather than 30, yet every 30 July source giving 72 also gives 30 unaccounted for, so 72 + 30 + 76 comes to 178 and not 179. The 73 figure reconciles; the 72 figure does not. The identification split of 68 and five has not been restated by anyone since 29 July.
